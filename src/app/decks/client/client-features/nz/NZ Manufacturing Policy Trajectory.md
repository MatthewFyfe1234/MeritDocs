# **A Strategic Analysis of New Zealand's Industrial Transition: Trajectory, Policy, and Regulatory Trajectory for the Manufacturing Sector to 2030**

The manufacturing sector in New Zealand represents a foundational component of the domestic economy, contributing approximately 8% of Gross Dependent Product (GDP), employing more than 220,000 workers, and accounting for nearly 60% of the nation's export earnings1. Historically characterized by a high proportion of small-to-medium enterprises (SMEs)—with 91% of manufacturing firms employing fewer than 20 staff—the sector is undergoing a profound transition driven by the coalition government's "Going for Growth" economic agenda3.  
This strategic analysis traces the regulatory, legislative, and fiscal pathways engineered by the government to restructure the economy. The overarching intent is to steer New Zealand manufacturing toward a highly productive, capital-intensive, digitally modernized, and export-competitive model. This report evaluates the statutory obligations, compliance pressures, and commercial incentives that will shape manufacturing operations through to 2030\.

## **Productivity and Economic Performance**

New Zealand has long suffered from a persistent labor productivity gap relative to other OECD countries, characterized by low output per worker5. Historically, economic growth has relied on labor utilization rather than capital accumulation, which has natural limits and acts as a drag on wage growth5. Government policy aims to address this challenge through two primary economic mechanisms: the diffusion of advanced technologies from the global frontier to domestic firms, and the reallocation of capital and labor from low-productivity businesses to high-productivity, export-oriented firms5.  
To incentivize capital-for-labor substitution, the government introduced the Investment Boost initiative in Budget 20256. This policy allows businesses, regardless of size, to immediately deduct 20% of the cost of a new eligible asset in the year of purchase, on top of standard depreciation6. The tax incentive applies to new physical assets purchased within New Zealand as well as new and used assets imported from overseas, including commercial buildings and advanced manufacturing equipment6. It excludes land, residential buildings, and assets already in use domestically6.  
Inland Revenue modeling indicates that the Investment Boost reduces the effective marginal tax rate on new capital investment by approximately five to six percentage points8. Long-term Treasury projections estimate that this initiative will lift New Zealand's GDP by 1%, real wages by 1.5%, and the capital stock by 1.6% over a 20-year horizon, with half of these gains expected to materialize by 20306.  
Manufacturers are expected to leverage these tax write-offs to invest in high-output machinery, automation, and industrial robotics1. By lowering the hurdle rate for capital expenditures, the government intends to shift manufacturing operations away from low-wage, manual-labor setups and transition the sector toward high-value manufacturing5.  
This transition is already yielding localized outcomes. In the March 2026 quarter, the manufacturing sector grew by 1.9%, outperforming the wider GDP growth of 0.8% and serving as the primary driver of national economic growth1. This growth was driven by high-tech manufacturing segments, including transport equipment, machinery, and specialized components1. Under Small Business and Manufacturing Minister Cameron Brewer, the government has focused on supporting advanced firms, such as Dawn Aerospace, Rocket Lab, and Fisher & Paykel Healthcare, while working with the Manufacturing Productivity Advisory Group and Advancing Manufacturing Aotearoa (AMA) to drive sector-wide productivity1.

## **Workforce Development and Vocational Training Settings**

Following a commitment to reverse the centralization of vocational education, the government passed legislation in October 2025 to disestablish the unified national entity, Te Pūkenga, effective 1 January 202610. The vocational training landscape has returned to a decentralized model to align skills development with regional labor market demands12.

| **Regional Polytechnics** | 10 independent regional polytechnics have been re-established to govern localized training10. Four remaining institutions operating under transitional arrangements must transition to new structures by 1 January 202712. |
| **Industry Skills Boards (ISBs)** | 8 newly established ISBs have replaced the former Workforce Development Councils to drive industry-led training standards and curriculum design10. |

The government's vocational strategy emphasizes secondary-to-workplace pathways and targeted skills funding. Budget 2026 allocated $15 million to enable ISBs to develop at least eight new industry-led secondary school subjects focused on specific technical fields14. Concurrently, the government committed $69 million to nearly double the number of Trades Academy places to 20,000 by 2030, aiming to expand practical, job-ready training14.  
This is supported by the restructuring of the Fees Free scheme, which transitioned on 1 January 2025 to cover up to $12,000 for the final year of provider-based study or the final two years of work-based learning, incentivizing program completion over initial enrollment15. To address immediate labor deficits, the Apprenticeship Boost scheme was prioritized in October 2024 to target critical occupations and skills shortages15.  
Under the new decentralized regime, manufacturers must take an active role in regional workforce planning16. Employers are expected to co-design training pathways, host work-integrated learning placements, and adopt structured mentorship models to address systemic coordination bottlenecks and supervision shortages on factory floors16. This is supported by regional initiatives like the Earn as You Learn pilot, which connects high school students with local manufacturers, providing paid, real-world experience during study18. The pilot achieved completion rates exceeding 80% in the Waikato region and is expanding to the Hutt Valley and Canterbury, with an Auckland launch scheduled for 202718.

## **Workplace Standards, Wages, and Fair Pay Settings**

The industrial relations framework has undergone significant statutory adjustments to enhance labor market flexibility and reduce compliance overheads for employers4.  
The Employment Relations Amendment Act 2026, which came into force on 21 February 2026 under Workplace Relations and Safety Minister Brooke van Velden, introduced major changes to individual and collective employment dynamics19. The legislation re-establishes 90-day trial periods for all employers, regardless of size, by removing the "30-day rule" and repealing corresponding union notification requirements4.  
This reform allows employers to negotiate individual terms with new non-union hires from day one of employment4. To provide greater commercial certainty, the Act amends Section 6 to clarify contracting arrangements, placing greater weight on the mutual intent of the parties4. Under the amended criteria, an individual is classified as an independent contractor if they are not required to work specific hours, can subcontract tasks, do not face contract termination upon refusing additional work, and have a reasonable opportunity to seek independent legal advice before signing4.  
The personal grievance (PG) system has also been adjusted to deter poor employee behavior and balance employer risks4. The 2026 Act rebalances the system by removing eligibility for remedies—including reinstatement or compensation for hurt and humiliation—if an employee's conduct constitutes serious misconduct4. Furthermore, the Employment Relations Authority and the Employment Court can apply remedy reductions of up to 100% if an employee's actions contributed to the grievance4.  
To reduce litigation risks for high-salary positions, the Act introduces a $200,000 annual remuneration threshold20. Employees earning above this threshold cannot bring unjustified dismissal personal grievances, subject to a 12-month transition period for existing employment agreements4. Complementing these changes, the Employment Relations (Pay Deductions for Partial Strikes) Amendment Act 2025 restored employers' ability to make proportional wage deductions in response to partial strike action, limiting the impact of low-level industrial disruptions on production lines19.  
The upcoming Holidays Act replacement, the Employment Leave Bill, was introduced in March 2026 and referred to the Education and Workforce Select Committee22. The Committee is scheduled to report back by 13 July 202623.  
The proposed framework replaces the legacy weeks- and days-based leave entitlement system with an hours-based accrual model, calculated from day one of employment24. Annual leave will accrue at a minimum statutory rate of 0.0769 hours per standard hour worked, while sick leave will accrue at 0.0385 hours per standard hour, up to a maximum cap of 160 hours25. Leave will be paid at the employee's lowest hourly wage rate for the day the leave is taken, simplifying calculations24.  
For casual employees and those working additional hours on top of their contracted standard hours, the Bill introduces a mandatory 12.5% upfront Leave Compensation Payment (LCP) in lieu of standard leave accrual23. The Bill proposes a 24-month implementation period following enactment to allow payroll providers and businesses to update their systems, alongside a 3-year transitional window for employers to align existing employment contracts with the new hours-based framework25.  
To resolve historical calculation errors under the 2003 Act, the Bill establishes a statutory estimation process for remediation calculations, shielding employers from line-by-line litigation where a valid estimate of arrears has been provided24.  
On pay equity and wage transparency, the government has shifted away from mandatory reporting requirements toward business-led initiatives. In October 2025, Women's Minister Nicola Grigg expanded the voluntary Gender Pay Gap Toolkit, introducing an online calculator to help businesses voluntarily measure and address gender, ethnic, and disability-related pay disparities27. Under this voluntary approach, New Zealand's national gender pay gap decreased to 5.2% in the June 2025 quarter, down from 8.2% in June 202427.

## **Health, Safety, and Worker Wellbeing**

The health and safety regulatory system is undergoing a major reform led by the Health and Safety at Work Amendment Bill, which was reported back from the Education and Workforce Select Committee on 16 June 202630. The legislation is scheduled to come into force on 1 November 2026, the day after receiving Royal Assent30. The central objective of the reform is to shift the regulatory focus from broad compliance administrative duties to the management of "critical risks"—specifically those high-consequence hazards that cause the most severe workplace injuries and fatalities31.  
The Bill introduces a statutory definition of a *small PCBU* (Person Conducting a Business or Undertaking), defined as an entity employing fewer than 20 workers for at least 9 out of 12 months in a financial year32. Under the amended Act, small PCBUs are only required to meet general health and safety duties (sections 36 to 38\) and upstream supply-chain duties (sections 39 to 43\) as they relate to *critical risks*33.  
"Critical risk" is defined as a hazard governed by specific high-risk regulations listed in the newly created Schedule 1A of the Act, alongside a principles-based catch-all for non-regulated risks likely to result in death, a notifiable injury or illness, a notifiable incident, or an occupational disease listed under Schedule 2 of the Accident Compensation Act 200132.  
Small PCBUs are only required to provide worker training, supervision, and personal protective equipment (PPE) for these scheduled critical risks33. For larger manufacturers, upstream duties under sections 39 to 43 remain fully active across all risks; however, if their client is a small PCBU, those design, manufacturing, and supply obligations are legally restricted to critical risks, helping to align safety expectations across the supply chain34.  
To reduce administrative friction, the Bill clarifies interactions with other regulatory regimes32. If a manufacturer complies with safety requirements under other relevant legislation—such as maritime safety rules or seismic risk standards in commercial buildings—they are legally deemed to have met their statutory duty under the HSWA, eliminating duplicative safety regulations31. Concurrently, the Bill aligns the statutory objectives of WorkSafe and other designated regulators to focus on supporting critical risk management rather than administrative compliance31.  
The legislation also strengthens the "safe harbour" status of Approved Codes of Practice (ACOPs), making it clear that following an ACOP is deemed compliance with the corresponding safety duty31. The Bill allows industry bodies to submit draft ACOPs directly to the regulator for approval32. Only the two most recent codes—the *Approved Code of Practice for Loading and Unloading Cargo at Ports and on Ships (2024)* and the *Approved Code of Practice: Safe Practice for Forestry and Harvesting Operations (2025)*—retain immediate safe harbour status, as older codes were not drafted with this statutory shield in mind32.  
Additionally, the government has simplified compliance in specialized settings; under changes to the Hazardous Substances regulations agreed in early 2026, research laboratories are exempt from standard handling certifications, and laboratory managers are no longer required to be on-site at all times or hold certified knowledge of every chemical used38.

| Regulatory Duty | Large PCBU Settings (\>= 20 Workers) | Small PCBU Settings (\< 20 Workers) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **General Duty of Care (Sections 36–38)** | Broad duty to manage all workplace risks and ensure health and safety so far as is reasonably practicable34. | Restricted solely to the management of "critical risks" that could cause death or serious injury/illness33. |
| **Upstream Supply Duties (Sections 39–43)** | Fully active across all design, manufacturing, importing, and installation activities34. | Restricted solely to the design, supply, and installation of plant and substances related to scheduled critical risks34. |

These health and safety reforms are highly contested by safety professionals, unions, and community advocates39. Critics, including the New Zealand Institute of Safety Management (NZISM) and the *Stand with Pike* campaign, argue that narrowing the statutory duties of small businesses—which make up 97% of domestic firms—will compromise worker safety in higher-risk segments, noting that small businesses have a 24% higher injury rate than large firms39.  
They point out that the carve-out excludes legal protections against musculoskeletal risks (such as ergonomics, slips, trips, and falls) and psychosocial hazards (such as workplace stress and mental fatigue), which represent the top two causes of workplace harm39. ACC has opposed the musculoskeletal carve-out, noting that it pays over $3.6 billion annually for such injuries in small businesses39.

## **Privacy and Employee Data Governance**

Under the Privacy Act 2020, manufacturers utilizing digital monitoring tools to track workforce performance must ensure that data collection is necessary for legitimate business functions and is not unreasonably intrusive41. Employers are legally required to establish clear workplace policies and obtain explicit employee consent before deploying digital tracking systems41. Covert monitoring remains restricted, except under strict, time-limited conditions such as active theft investigations, and must cease once the immediate cause is resolved43.  
The deployment of biometrics is governed by the Privacy Commissioner’s guidelines44. Under Rule 10 of the Biometrics Code, tracking worker fatigue, attention, or alertness using cameras or biometric categorisation is permitted under exceptions to prevent or lessen a threat to life or health in high-risk operational environments, such as heavy machinery operation44.  
However, using biometric tracking to monitor productivity, stress, or emotional reactions in standard work environments is generally prohibited, as these applications do not meet the safety exception threshold44. Similarly, GPS vehicle tracking requires clear policies and a strict separation of work and non-work data to avoid infringing on personal privacy during off-duty hours45.  
These data protections operate alongside the Customer and Product Data Act 2025, which establishes a statutory framework for consumer data rights and open data portability across the economy46. While the banking sector is the first designated under this regime, the government is drafting regulations to designate the retail electricity sector next, with an estimated launch date of 1 July 202746.  
This regime requires designated data holders to share customer data in standardized, machine-readable formats with accredited third parties upon request46. Although these reforms do not directly govern employee records, they establish a precedent for data interoperability and security that will influence digital operations across the manufacturing supply chain.

## **Decarbonisation and Emissions**

The New Zealand Emissions Trading Scheme (NZ ETS) serves as the government's primary market mechanism to meet its international commitments under the Paris Agreement and progress toward the legislated 2050 Net Zero greenhouse gas target48. By capping total allowable emissions, the ETS places a direct price on carbon across approximately 40% of the economy (excluding agriculture), incentivizing industrial decarbonization48.  
To protect export competitiveness and prevent "emissions leakage"—where production shifts to jurisdictions without carbon pricing—the government continues to provide free industrial allocations of NZUs to emissions-intensive, trade-exposed (EITE) manufacturers48.  
Under the ETS settings for 2026–2030, the government reduced the volume of New Zealand Units (NZUs) available for auction to reduce the historical market surplus, which has decreased to approximately 50 million units50. This cap adjustment aims to drive private sector investment in low-emission alternatives, such as converting coal-fired boilers to biomass or electricity, reducing reliance on fossil gas, and expanding renewable energy systems52.  
However, because industrial allocations have been lower than expected due to plant closures, reduced production, and updated baselines, the Climate Change Commission advised that the government can auction 13.6 million more units over the 2028–2030 period while remaining within the legislated emissions budgets51.  
Concurrently, the government has modified the Climate-Related Disclosures (CRD) regime through the Financial Markets Conduct Amendment Bill, which was reported back from the Finance and Expenditure Committee on 30 January 202653. These changes aim to reduce compliance costs for mid-sized entities while maintaining reporting requirements for the nation's largest institutions54.

* **Reporting Threshold Raise:** The mandatory climate reporting threshold for listed companies has been raised from a market capitalization of $60 million to $1 billion54. This adjustment reduces the number of reporting entities from approximately 164 to 76, removing 66 listed companies and 22 Managed Investment Scheme (MIS) managers from the mandatory regime54.  
* **Evidentiary and Liability Relief:** The statutory changes ease liability settings for directors, removing automatic, deemed liability for certain entity-level disclosure breaches and lowering the standard of evidence required for forward-looking statements55.  
* **Safe Harbour for Compliance:** To encourage transparent disclosures, the Bill inserts a new section 26A, which removes the prohibition on making unsubstantiated representations for Climate Reporting Entities, provided they comply with climate standards in all material respects57.

To provide additional operational breathing room, the Financial Markets Authority (FMA) extended the temporary exemption on mandatory Scope 3 emissions assurance for an additional two years58. The exemption now applies to accounting periods ending before 31 December 2027, with the principal notice scheduled for revocation on 30 April 202858. This extension acknowledges that Scope 3 disclosures—which track indirect upstream and downstream value chain emissions—face ongoing challenges regarding data availability and reliability, providing additional time for organizations to develop reliable data-sharing structures with their suppliers58.

## **Embodied Carbon and Materials**

The government's long-term environmental framework aims to achieve "near zero carbon" buildings by 2050 through the Building for Climate Change (BfCC) programme60. This strategy targets whole-of-life carbon performance, evaluating both operational carbon (energy use during a building's lifespan) and embodied carbon (emissions from material extraction, manufacturing, transport, and construction waste)60. Under the Building Code, the whole-of-life embodied carbon reduction framework establishes reporting requirements to measure and cap material emissions over time60.  
To support these calculations, manufacturers of construction materials are encouraged to use Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) tools, such as BRANZ's *LCAQuick* and *LCAPlay*, and publish verified Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs)60. The government also actively supports the use of low-carbon materials, such as mass timber, where they can demonstrably reduce a project's carbon footprint62.  
While the government supports the use of low-carbon materials, it has adjusted public sector procurement rules to reduce administrative requirements63. Under the 5th edition of the Government Procurement Rules, the government removed the requirement to use an approved sustainable building rating system (such as Green Star) for new non-residential government-owned buildings over $9 million63. This change replaces centralized sustainability mandates with a localized "economic benefit" model, giving agencies greater flexibility to evaluate environmental and social outcomes based on project-specific public value64.

## **Waste, Product Stewardship, and the Circular Economy**

The government’s waste minimization strategy aims to transition New Zealand toward a circular economy by addressing product design and material use alongside downstream waste management65. Under the 2024 amendments to the Waste Minimisation Act 2008, the statutory scope of the waste disposal levy was expanded to fund central government environmental initiatives66. Concurrently, central government funding allocations to the contestable Waste Minimisation Fund (WMF) have been capped at $30 million per annum, and the Contaminated Sites & Vulnerable Landfills Fund (CSVLF) has been capped at $20 million per annum66.  
To offset these baseline funding adjustments, the central government portion of levy revenues has been redirected to cover environmental and regulatory work programs within the Ministry for the Environment, the Ministry for Primary Industries, and the Department of Conservation66. This change enabled $232.5 million in fiscal savings to the Crown in Budget 2025, including $44.6 million for the Ministry for the Environment's waste and hazardous substances work program and $40 million for the remediation of contaminated sites66.  
To disincentivize waste and fund environmental outcomes, the government has scheduled increases to the waste disposal levy rate over the next three years, which will increase landfill disposal costs for households and businesses66.  
To address lifecycle waste, the government is transitioning voluntary recycling schemes into regulated, mandatory Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) systems67. Following the operational launch of the *Tyrewise* tire stewardship scheme on 1 September 2024, the government approved the drafting of regulations for the *Rural Recycling Scheme* in late 202569.  
This mandatory scheme requires producers of agrichemicals, plastic containers, farm plastics, silage wrap, and agricultural bags over 16kg to declare their sales volumes and pay regulated fees, typically ranging from 0.2% to 1.7% of product value71. These fees fund a nationwide collection and recycling network managed by the Agrecovery Foundation, providing agricultural and industrial end-users with free drop-off sites and regional collections71.  
Similar mandatory schemes are being designed for large batteries, electrical waste (e-waste), and commercial packaging67. In December 2024, the government also agreed to draft regulations for a mandatory product stewardship scheme for synthetic refrigerant gases to phase out high-GWP gases, with progressive implementation scheduled through to 203068.

To support industry adjustments, the government has revised its timelines for single-use plastic restrictions67. The 2025 deadline for the third tranche of plastic phase-outs—which target remaining polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and polystyrene food and beverage packaging—was removed to allow more time to develop alternative materials67. Additionally, the government amended the Waste Minimisation (Plastic and Related Products) Regulations 2022 to extend the compliance deadline for produce labels to 1 July 2028, ensuring all produce labels and their adhesives sold in New Zealand are certified home compostable68.

## **Export Competitiveness and Trade**

New Zealand manufacturers face indirect compliance pressures from international regulatory developments, which affect their access to premium global markets73. The European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (EU CBAM) entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026, imposing a carbon tariff on carbon-intensive imports like cement, iron, steel, aluminium, fertilisers, and hydrogen73.  
NZ exporters must calculate and report direct (Scope 1\) and indirect (Scope 2\) embedded emissions using the EU's approved methodology, though they can deduct any carbon price already paid domestically under the NZ ETS73.  
This is matched by the United Kingdom's CBAM, scheduled for implementation in January 202775. The UK scheme uses a self-assessment tax model and extends coverage to ceramics and glass, but excludes the electricity sector75. While agricultural and food products are currently excluded from both the EU and UK CBAM regimes, the European Commission is evaluating plans to expand the scope to transport and downstream manufactured components75.  
To support New Zealand's target of doubling export values by 2035, the government allocated $84 million in Budget 2025 to expand trade engagement, address non-tariff barriers, and assist exporters in navigating international regulatory frameworks77. Exporters can access compliance guidance, supply-chain analysis, and market-entry support through joint programs run by New Zealand Trade and Enterprise (NZTE) and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade (MFAT)73.

## **Digital Transformation and Business Modernisation**

To address productivity challenges, the government expanded the Digital Manufacturing Light (DM Light) initiative9. Developed in partnership with the University of Auckland and Auckland Council, the program is adapted from the University of Cambridge’s "Digital Manufacturing on a Shoestring" model9.  
Beginning 1 April 2026, the government committed up to $475,000 per year over three years to expand the program, aiming to support at least 180 small-to-medium manufacturers across Auckland, Waikato, Northland, and the Bay of Plenty (where 55% of New Zealand's manufacturers are located)9. The program utilizes low-cost, off-the-shelf digital tools and open-source software to integrate digital technologies into existing factory operations, providing real-time data on machine performance, bottlenecks, and quality control9.

## **Government Procurement**

The 5th Edition of the Government Procurement Rules came into force on 1 December 202582. The updated rules apply to approximately 140 government agencies and govern over $50 billion in annual public spending61. The primary objective of the revision is to simplify procurement processes, reduce compliance overheads for bidding businesses, and leverage public spending to support domestic economic growth63.  
The 5th edition replaces the "broader outcomes" rules of the 4th edition with a simplified rule: *Economic Benefit to New Zealand*64. The rule requires purchasing agencies to evaluate how a contract will generate tangible economic activity within New Zealand82. This evaluation includes assessing opportunities for local employment, skills development, workforce training, and industrial growth, particularly for regional businesses and SMEs83.  
All government procurements that meet or exceed the threshold of $100,000 for goods and services, or $9 million for construction works, must include a *minimum 10% evaluation weighting* on these domestic economic benefit criteria63.  
To make it easier for domestic businesses to compete for contracts, the 5th edition reduced the total number of procurement rules from 71 to 47, removing redundant or duplicate requirements63. The rules establish stricter standards for panel contracts and secondary procurement, and require agencies to publish their internal procurement policies and contract award notices to improve transparency82. This framework allows agencies to incorporate social and environmental criteria into contracts, provided these criteria align with public value and deliver a clear economic benefit to New Zealand64.

The New Zealand government's policy framework through to 2030 establishes clear priorities, timelines, and operational expectations for the manufacturing sector.

### **Immediate Strategic Priorities for New Zealand Manufacturers**

The immediate challenges for manufacturers center on labor relations, workplace safety, and capital deployment:

1. **Critical Risk Safety Governance:** With the *Health and Safety at Work Amendment Act* commencing on 1 November 2026, manufacturers must align their risk registers with the new focus on scheduled critical risks30. Large manufacturers must review their upstream design and supply-chain liabilities, particularly when supplying small businesses that are exempt from non-critical compliance duties34.  
2. **Employment Standards and Leave Systems:** Bidding and payroll systems must prepare for the hours-based leave calculations and 12.5% Leave Compensation Payments introduced by the *Employment Leave Bill*24. While the bill provides a 24-month window for system implementation, manufacturers must review their employment contracts to ensure alignment25.  
3. **Capital Strategy and Modernization:** Manufacturers should utilize the *Investment Boost* to write off upfront capital costs for advanced machinery and automation6. This investment addresses the sector's long-term labor shortages and aligns with the government's transition away from labor-intensive setups5.

### **Areas of Regulatory Intensification Before 2030**

Over the medium term, regulatory pressure will increase in the areas of emissions reporting, waste management, and data transparency:

* **Carbon and Supply Chain Disclosures:** Although the domestic CRD reporting threshold has been raised to $1 billion, mid-sized and small manufacturers face indirect pressure to report emissions54. Large financial entities and international clients will increasingly require Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions data to satisfy their own reporting and CBAM compliance requirements58.  
* **Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) and Waste Costs:** As scheduled increases to the waste disposal levy take effect, landfill costs will rise66. The rollout of mandatory product stewardship schemes for plastics, packaging, and industrial batteries will require manufacturers to integrate lifecycle recovery costs directly into product pricing67.  
* **Data Portability and Security:** The progressive expansion of the *Customer and Product Data Act 2025* into energy and other utility sectors will require manufacturers to adopt secure, standardized data practices46. This will demand increased digital capability to ensure compliance with the Privacy Commissioner's guidelines on biometrics and data access44.

### **Profile of a High-Performing New Zealand Manufacturer in 2030**

The manufacturers best positioned to succeed under this policy regime will share key operational characteristics:

* **Capital-Intensive and Automated:** They will have transitioned from low-wage, manual operations to capital-intensive production structures, leveraging the *Investment Boost* and *RDTI* in-year payments to integrate automation, industrial robotics, and AI6.  
* **Digitally Integrated and Data-Driven:** They will use real-time digital monitoring and cloud computing to manage factory workflows, track energy use, and maintain compliance with privacy standards9.  
* **Carbon-Transparent:** They will maintain verified metrics for Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions, supported by Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs), allowing them to navigate carbon tariffs like the EU CBAM and secure preferred status in corporate supply chains58.  
* **Safety-Focused on Critical Risks:** They will operate under robust safety cultures that align with WorkSafe's critical risk priorities, utilizing Approved Codes of Practice (ACOPs) as safe harbors to minimize compliance overheads31.  
* **Aligned with Domestic Procurement Values:** They will structure their public tenders to clearly demonstrate New Zealand economic benefits, such as regional employment, skills development, and local material use, securing a competitive advantage under the 5th Edition Procurement Rules82.
