# **The New Zealand Frame and Truss Manufacturing Sector: An In-Depth Structural and Economic Analysis**

## **Sector Size, Structure, and Industry Demography**

The New Zealand timber frame and truss manufacturing sector is a highly industrialized, core component of the national residential construction supply chain. Demographically, the sector operates under several specific classifications within the Australian and New Zealand Standard Industrial Classification (ANZSIC 2006\) system, primarily Wood Product Manufacturing (such as ANZSIC C1491: Prefabricated Wooden Building Manufacturing, and ANZSIC C1499: Wood Product Manufacturing Not Elsewhere Classified).  
According to Statistics New Zealand's annual business demography database, last updated on October 30, 2025, and published via Figure.NZ ([https://figure.nz/chart/IW0xisEu03j4YW09-cLbkWHaimcW2insj](https://figure.nz/chart/IW0xisEu03j4YW09-cLbkWHaimcW2insj)), the number of economically significant enterprise units in the Prefabricated Wooden Building Manufacturing industry (C1491) has steadily expanded over the past twenty-five years.1 An enterprise represents a legal business entity—such as a registered limited company or partnership—with annual sales or expenses subject to Goods and Services Tax (GST) exceeding $30,000, or a rolling 12-month mean employee count of more than three.1

The structural composition of the sector is highly bifurcated between a small group of large-scale industrial operations and a vast network of micro-enterprises.2 The MPI survey frame identified approximately 8,000 total active firms in forestry and wood processing, yet nearly 7,000 of these registered five or fewer employees, or zero employees.2 True industrial scale and workforce participation are concentrated in approximately 1,000 core firms, of which 40% record an annual turnover exceeding $1 million.2 Peer-reviewed research reviewing the prefabrication sector in New Zealand identified 182 manufacturers of prefabricated buildings and components nationwide.3 Within this cohort, 72 dedicated frame and truss plants were registered with the Frame and Truss Manufacturers' Association (FTMA) NZ in 2016, with the remaining firms consisting of highly specialized regional prefabricators of structural components (trusses, wall frames, and panels) rather than completed modular building manufacturers.3

## **Market Economics, Concentration, and Vertical Integration**

The economic dynamics of the frame and truss sector are heavily shaped by structural concentration at the retail merchant level and a high degree of vertical integration across the residential building material supply chain. Most structural timber and prefabricated framing components are distributed to builders through a highly consolidated retail merchant network.4 This network is dominated by five major players with national footprints: Bunnings, Carters, ITM, Mitre 10, and PlaceMakers.4  
The Commerce Commission’s *Residential Building Supplies Market Study Final Report* (published on December 6, 2022, available at [https://www.comcom.govt.nz/\_\_data/assets/pdf\_file/0013/300703/Residential-building-supplies-market-study-Executive-summary-6-December-2022.pdf](https://www.comcom.govt.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0013/300703/Residential-building-supplies-market-study-Executive-summary-6-December-2022.pdf)) highlighted that vertical integration within this network has created significant structural barriers to entry and expansion.5 For instance, Fletcher Building represents a major vertically integrated group, manufacturing key products such as plasterboard (via Winstone Wallboards GIB), cement (Golden Bay Cement), ready-mix concrete (Firth), and glass wool insulation (Tasman Insulation), while distributing these materials through its national trade retail merchant network, PlaceMakers.4  
In 2022, the Commerce Commission granted clearance for Fletcher Distribution Limited (FDL), the operating company of PlaceMakers, to acquire six building products stores and a frame and truss manufacturing plant from Tumu Merchants Limited in the Wairarapa and Hawke's Bay regions.6 While the Commission concluded this specific transaction would not substantially lessen competition due to the local presence of Carters, ITM, Bunnings, and Mitre 10, it underscores the ongoing consolidation of local frame and truss plants into major vertically integrated distribution systems.6  
This consolidated market structure directly influences commercial pricing dynamics. The Commerce Commission's 2022 study revealed that major established manufacturers use quantity-forcing rebate structures to lock in merchant supply lines.4 These rebates offer higher percentage savings that apply retrospectively across all of a merchant’s purchases with a supplier once specific volume thresholds are met.4 Under certain conditions, these rebates deter merchants from stocking alternative or innovative structural timber and component products, severely limiting the ability of independent prefabricators or imported product suppliers to scale.5  
On the supply side, frame and truss manufacturers face intense material cost inflation.7 Since 2021, building material prices in New Zealand have escalated by an average of 45%, contributing significantly to domestic inflationary pressures.7 Compounding these material costs is New Zealand's unique architectural "measure up" culture, which favors highly bespoke design layouts over standardized structural dimensions.8  
Independent advocacy submissions to the Commerce Commission, such as those by Monopoly Watch NZ in March 2022, argue that this bespoke culture creates significant structural inefficiencies.8 Under this model, frame and truss plants function as highly customized, small-batch fabricators rather than mass-market producers, leading to higher labor costs, more design-office processing time, and increased price volatility for end consumers.8 To illustrate this localized high-cost setting, comparative structural studies cite the installed price of basic lining products: plasterboard installed in Auckland costs approximately $5.70 per square meter, compared to $3.35 per square meter in Brisbane, directly reflecting the compounding costs of bespoke building layouts and localized monopoly settings.8

## **Workforce Competencies, Apprenticeship Pipelines, and Labour Availability**

The transition of the frame and truss manufacturing sector toward advanced automated production is closely tied to the technical capabilities and availability of its workforce. The primary vocational training pathways for the sector are developed and managed by the Building and Construction Industry Training Organisation (BCITO).9 These credentials focus on two core professional career paths: timber structure manufacturing and digital detailing.9

These apprenticeships are completed full-time on-the-job under the supervision of an experienced employer, supported by a dedicated BCITO training advisor.9 The Level 5 Detailing qualification represents a critical technical step-up, moving workers from manual machine operation to advanced software coordination.9 Detailers use CAD-based proprietary software to translate architectural plans into high-precision cutting lists and digital layouts, which are then fed directly to factory saws and assembly jigs.9  
Despite the structured training system, labor availability remains a significant bottleneck.12 Academic reviews and industry feedback highlight that the New Zealand construction sector has suffered from persistent, systemic skills shortages, which have severely limited the industry's capacity to adopt advanced off-site manufacturing (OSM) technologies.12 This is supported by the 2021 MPI Labour Force Survey, which identified extensive workforce shortages that forced wood processing firms to turn away work.2 The shortage is most acute in manual operations and skilled machine operators.2  
Because vocational education providers struggle to offer standardized off-site manufacturing training, frame and truss plants must invest heavily in internal upskilling to maintain quality and safety standards.12 When market activity peaks, the combination of tight delivery deadlines and high staff turnover often forces plants to operate with a high proportion of semi-skilled or under-trained personnel, directly impacting operational safety and quality control.13

## **Occupational Health and Safety Profile**

The physical hazards associated with structural timber fabrication are pronounced, given the widespread use of high-speed automated cross-cut saws, pneumatic presses, and nail guns.9 In 2024, the broader New Zealand construction sector recorded 31,200 new Accident Compensation Corporation (ACC) claims—accounting for 15% of all workplace injury claims nationwide—and suffered 12 workplace fatalities.14  
Within the timber manufacturing sector, the most detailed epidemiological profile of injuries is provided by the Scion analysis of accepted ACC claims (published in 2007, available at [https://scion.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/api/collection/p20044coll14/id/47/download](https://scion.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/api/collection/p20044coll14/id/47/download)).15 Out of an initial dataset of 12,133 claims, Scion analyzed 11,124 claims accepted over a two-year period (January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2006\) specifically in the timber processing industries.15

The Scion report highlights several key safety issues for the frame and truss sector:

* **Severe Hand Injuries:** The Wooden Structural Component sector accounted for 18% of all finger and thumb injury claims in the wood processing industry, largely due to high-risk contact with nail guns and saws.15  
* **High Entitlement Claims:** Within the Wooden Structural Component sector, 18.9% (342 claims) were classified as "Entitlement" claims—meaning they required significant compensation, rehabilitation, or weekly compensation for more than a week off work.15 The remaining 74.2% (1,340 claims) were for medical fees.15  
* **Demographics and Accreditation:** Only 3% of claimants in the structural component manufacturing sub-sector were female, and 99% of claimants were employed by employers who were not accredited under ACC’s partnership program.15  
* **Sector Incident Rates:** For the 200 calendar year, the overall wood processing injury incidence rate was 211 claims per 1,000 Full-Time Equivalents (FTEs).15 The "Other Wood Product Manufacturing" category (which includes structural components) recorded an incidence rate of 222 claims per 1,000 FTEs.15 In comparison, log sawmilling recorded 264 claims per 1,000 FTEs.15

Comparative international safety data published by forest agencies shows that while wood product manufacturing suffers from a high volume of ergonomic and physical injuries, the forestry and logging sub-sectors demonstrate nearly twice as many fatal accidents per capita, highlighting the highly manual nature of raw log extraction compared to factory-controlled fabrication.17  
Historically, high-demand environments create a strong incentive for staff to work long hours and rush production, which leads to fatigue and increased injuries.13 To mitigate this risk, the Construction Growth Foundation (CGF) funded a major health and safety video series in 2024 in partnership with the FTMA.14 This project targeted younger and less experienced workers, providing quick, visual, and accessible on-the-job safety guidelines for high-risk power tools, including circular saws, reciprocating saws, electric planers, and impact drivers.14

## **Regulatory Frameworks and Compliance Burden**

The New Zealand building regulatory framework has undergone substantial shifts, introducing new voluntary pathways while updating existing standards, which has changed the compliance burden on frame and truss manufacturers. The primary acceptable solution for timber framing remains NZS 3604: Timber-framed buildings, a standard that frame and truss plants use to demonstrate structural compliance during the building consent process.18  
To support the uptake of off-site manufacturing, the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE) established the **BuiltReady Modular Component Manufacturer Scheme** under the Building (Modular Component Manufacturer Scheme) Regulations 2022\.20 This voluntary scheme, which commenced on September 7, 2022, and opened for manufacturer applications in 2024, provides a "deemed-to-comply" pathway.21 Certified and registered manufacturers can issue manufacturer’s certificates for modular components.20 Under Section 19 of the Building Act, Building Consent Authorities (BCAs) must accept these certificates as evidence of compliance with the Building Code, bypassing standard regional inspection delays.20  
The BuiltReady scheme defines three types of modular components:

1. **Prefabricated Frames and Panels:** Open or enclosed frames, trusses, floor, wall, and ceiling cassettes, or structural insulated panels (SIPs).20  
2. **Prefabricated Volumetric Structures:** Three-dimensional modules, such as bathroom or laundry pods.20  
3. **Prefabricated Whole Buildings:** Complete off-site manufactured buildings, excluding foundations and site work.20

While the BuiltReady scheme offers a faster path to building consents, it also introduces a significant administrative and financial compliance burden.25 Certification requires manufacturers to establish rigorous Quality Management Systems (QMS) compliant with ISO/IEC 17065:2013, implement formal internal audit programs, and pass fit-and-proper person and financial viability assessments.24

The financial benefit of the BuiltReady pathway is directly tied to avoiding construction delays. MBIE estimates that a single week of delay on the construction of a new residential dwelling costs the developer approximately $2,047.29 Under BuiltReady, if a certified manufacturer designs and manufactures a whole building, the BCA is legally required to process the building consent application within 10 working days, significantly mitigating these delay costs.25  
The FTMA lobbied extensively during the design phase of the modular component scheme.18 The association expressed concern that standard frame and truss plants might be forced to register under the new scheme, which would impose unnecessary compliance costs.18 MBIE later clarified that the BuiltReady scheme is voluntary, and standard frame and truss documentation remains fully compliant under existing acceptable solutions.18 Most traditional frame and truss manufacturers continue to operate outside the BuiltReady framework, finding standard NZS 3604 pathways more cost-effective for localized markets.18  
Additionally, frame and truss plants face ongoing adjustments to the Building Code, such as the H1 Energy Efficiency updates.18 These updates increased the insulation performance (R-values) required for new residential dwellings.18 While the thermal performance changes have a minor direct impact on standard framing fabrication, they have required closer coordination with designers to accommodate thicker wall cavities and deeper ceiling installations.18 Proposed updates to increase Fire Resistance Ratings (FRRs) in medium-density developments have also drawn concern from the FTMA, who argue that excessive technical compliance costs provide little benefit for simple timber-framed designs.18

## **Housing Demand Context, Construction Pipeline, and the Role of Prefabrication**

Understanding the New Zealand housing market requires separating long-term demand from the short-term construction pipeline. While general commentary often focuses on housing shortages, specific quantified data regarding the historical or current housing unit deficit in New Zealand is unverified and unavailable in the provided primary source materials. Therefore, this analysis refrains from making ungrounded estimates to maintain statistical integrity.  
However, the forward construction pipeline is highly documented. According to MBIE’s *National Construction Pipeline Report 2025* (published in 2025, available at [https://www.mbie.govt.nz/building-and-energy/building/supporting-a-skilled-and-productive-workforce/national-construction-pipeline-report](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/building-and-energy/building/supporting-a-skilled-and-productive-workforce/national-construction-pipeline-report)) and its companion infographic ([https://www.mbie.govt.nz/dmsdocument/31694-national-construction-pipeline-report-2025-infographic](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/dmsdocument/31694-national-construction-pipeline-report-2025-infographic)), total construction activity is transitioning through a distinct cyclical downturn.30  
Total national construction activity is projected to fall to a low of $55.7 billion in the 2025 calendar year, down from $58.1 billion in 2024 and $63.0 billion in 2023\.30 Similarly, the value of residential construction activity is forecast to decrease from $29.2 billion in 2024 to a low of $26.1 billion in 2025, before starting a recovery from 2026 onward to finish at $32.3 billion in 2030\.30

The residential construction pipeline is marked by a major structural shift: multi-unit dwellings (such as townhouses, duplexes, and apartments) are projected to account for 115,000 of the 215,000 total dwellings consented over the next six years, with their share rising to 56% by 2030\.30  
Regionally, the forward pipeline is highly uneven. By 2030, Auckland will remain the largest market, accounting for 44% of all building consents.30 In contrast, Wellington's residential activity is projected to contract further through 2027 before recovering slightly to finish at $1.9 billion in 2030\.30 Canterbury is forecast to decline from early 2026, dropping to $3.7 billion (24.7% below 2023 values), while Otago's residential activity will contract steadily to reach $1.7 billion by 2030\.30  
This shift toward medium-density housing directly impacts frame and truss manufacturers. Detached single-family homes, which typically use standard wall frames and roof trusses, are increasingly being replaced by complex multi-unit configurations.18 These developments require specialized inter-tenancy walls, complex floor cassettes, and acoustic and fire-rated panel assemblies.9  
To capture this market, frame and truss plants must adapt their production lines from simple framing to complex, engineered components.9 The FTMA has pointed out that New Zealand already possesses significant unused manufacturing capacity.32 Off-site prefabrication of timber framing has been a standard building practice in New Zealand since the 1960s, and the association argues that the most efficient way to meet the country's housing demand is to fully utilize existing factory sites rather than over-investing in new, capital-intensive manufacturing facilities.32

## **Operational Performance, Waste Mitigation, and Productivity Dynamics**

The construction sector is a major waste producer in New Zealand, contributing up to 50% of all waste sent to landfills and cleanfills.33 BRANZ estimates that building a typical standalone house generates approximately 4 tonnes of waste, sending more than $31,000 worth of discarded materials to landfill per home.33 This high waste volume is driven by on-site estimation errors, damage during transport, and extensive off-cuts from manual site fabrication.34  
Transitioning construction from on-site assembly to off-site manufacturing (OSM) dramatically improves material efficiency and productivity.12 In factory-controlled environments, plants can implement Design for Manufacture and Assembly (DFMA) concepts.35 These systems allow designs to be sent directly from a CAD program to automated saws, which optimize cutting layouts to minimize timber off-cuts.35  
This material efficiency is supported by peer-reviewed research and BRANZ reports. In a 2014 study on prefabrication and standardization (Study Report SR 312, available at [https://d39d3mj7qio96p.cloudfront.net/media/documents/SR312\_Prefabrication\_and\_standardisation\_potential\_in\_buildings.pdf](https://d39d3mj7qio96p.cloudfront.net/media/documents/SR312_Prefabrication_and_standardisation_potential_in_buildings.pdf)), researchers Ian Page and David Norman estimated that New Zealand's standard wall and roof framing prefabrication sector was worth approximately $2.95 billion per year.12 They calculated that expanding standardization and off-site manufacturing to other building types and components could increase this value to more than $5 billion per year.12  
Academic and environmental assessments show that utilizing factory-controlled prefabrication can reduce construction waste by up to 40%, lower carbon dioxide emissions by 35%, and reduce energy consumption by 55% over a building's lifecycle compared to traditional site-built methods.12  
From a macro productivity perspective, even minor efficiency gains in construction have a major impact on the wider economy. A 1% increase in construction labor productivity is estimated to generate an additional $139 million in national Gross Domestic Product (GDP).12 Off-site frame and truss prefabrication is highly efficient because it minimizes on-site assembly hours, reduces rework from human error, and limits weather-related construction delays.12

## **Synthesis and Strategic Industry Outlook**

The New Zealand frame and truss manufacturing sector is entering a transitional phase. It faces a cyclical construction downturn in 2025, but a projected recovery toward 2030 will require adapting to a housing market increasingly dominated by medium-density, multi-unit developments.30  
The traditional model of New Zealand residential construction—characterized by bespoke, "measure-up" on-site framing—is highly inefficient.8 This model contributes to the 4 tonnes of waste generated per standalone house and the $31,000 worth of materials sent to landfill per build.33 Transitioning the industry toward off-site prefabrication offers clear benefits: factory-controlled fabrication can reduce construction waste by up to 40% and support a 1% increase in labor productivity, which would add $139 million to national GDP.12  
However, the sector faces several key challenges that limit its growth:

1. **Vertical Integration and Rebates:** The high concentration of the retail merchant network and the use of retrospective quantity-forcing rebates make it difficult for independent frame and truss prefabricators to scale and enter new markets.5  
2. **The Detailing Skills Gap:** As manufacturing processes become more automated, the industry faces a critical shortage of skilled detailers.2 Training workers to use advanced CAD and BIM detailing software (under the BCITO Level 5 Detailing qualification) is a slow process, which could create a bottleneck during the projected 2026–2030 construction recovery.11  
3. **The Compliance Cost of BuiltReady:** The BuiltReady modular component scheme provides a valuable, fast-track consenting pathway.20 However, the high cost of JASANZ accreditation and MBIE registration acts as a barrier for mid-tier, independent frame and truss operators, keeping the scheme restricted to large, highly capitalized off-site manufacturers.18

To address these challenges, the industry should focus on:

* **Promoting Standardization:** Moving away from the traditional "measure up" culture toward standardized component dimensions to reduce design-office times and lower labor costs.8  
* **Investing in Training:** Expanding support for BCITO qualifications to build a stronger pipeline of skilled machine operators and digital detailers.10  
* **Refining Regulatory Pathways:** Working with regulators to reduce the compliance costs of schemes like BuiltReady, making them more accessible to mid-tier manufacturers.25

By addressing these structural, workforce, and regulatory challenges, the New Zealand frame and truss sector can fully leverage its manufacturing capacity, drive construction productivity, and support the transition toward a more sustainable, lower-waste building industry.12
