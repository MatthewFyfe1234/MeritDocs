# **Structural and Demographic Audit of the New Zealand Frame and Truss Manufacturing Sector**

## **Enterprise Demographics and Class C1491 Classifications**

The New Zealand prefabricated structural timber component manufacturing sector is classified under the Australian and New Zealand Standard Industrial Classification (ANZSIC 2006\) system under Class C1491 (Prefabricated Wooden Building Manufacturing).1 This specific industry class comprises economically significant enterprises predominantly engaged in the offsite manufacturing of prefabricated wooden buildings, wall frames, roof trusses, and other structural timber elements.1  
According to primary business demography data compiled by Statistics New Zealand (Stats NZ), there were exactly 120 active economically significant enterprises operating within ANZSIC Class C1491 in New Zealand as of February 2024\.1 This figure remained stable with exactly 120 enterprises recorded as of February 2025\.1 This count reflects a period of long-term consolidation and subsequent expansion within the offsite manufacturing sector over the last two decades.1 To be categorized as an economically significant enterprise within the national Business Register, a business must meet at least one of several criteria, including annual expenses or sales subject to Goods and Services Tax (GST) exceeding NZD 30,000, a rolling 12-month mean employee count greater than three, or direct registration under a multi-enterprise corporate group.1

The claim that the average number of employees per business in the New Zealand frame and truss manufacturing sector is 7.2 cannot be validated using a primary New Zealand source.1 Fine-level employee counts by specific four-digit ANZSIC classes are withheld in public national summaries due to confidentiality protocols, administrative lags on the national Business Register, and classification discrepancies for multi-location enterprises.1  
However, trans-Tasman comparison data provides relevant context.4 According to industry data compiled for the identical ANZSIC Class C1491 in Australia, the average prefabricated wooden building manufacturing business employed exactly 7.8 workers as of 2025 and 2026\.4 This Australian sector registered a total of 1,161 employed persons across its enterprise pool.4 The structural similarities in residential construction methodologies between Australia and New Zealand indicate that the claimed average of 7.2 employees is consistent with the small-to-medium enterprise (SME) nature of New Zealand's regional frame and truss plants, although it remains officially unverified in New Zealand's national databases.

## **Structural Materials Market Capitalization and Setup Cost Dynamics**

The claim that New Zealand’s total structural materials market size is approximately NZD 8 billion cannot be validated by a direct, primary source. The physical value of building materials is typically analyzed across broader segments in national accounts.5 In the Commerce Commission's Residential Building Supplies Market Study, raw building materials were estimated to comprise between 16% and 24% of the total cost of residential housing development, or between 23% and 33% if land and infrastructure costs are excluded.6 At the supplier and merchant level, the absolute scale of the dominant market participant, Fletcher Building, is significant, with its manufacturing and distribution empire generating more than NZD 7 billion in total annual revenue.7  
Furthermore, research by the Building Research Association of New Zealand (BRANZ) in Study Report SR 312 provides a comprehensive assessment of standardisation and prefabrication potential within the domestic building sector.8 The study estimates that the potential for prefabrication could be scaled to more than NZD 5.0 billion per year, with an additional NZD 2.7 billion in potential standardisation benefits.8 While these two values overlap and are not directly additive, BRANZ indicates that the combined economic footprint of prefabricated and standardized construction components lies between NZD 5.0 billion and NZD 7.7 billion annually.8 This range aligns closely with the NZD 8 billion market estimate, suggesting that the NZD 8 billion figure represents the total addressable market for offsite fabrication and structural building components, rather than timber frame and truss manufacturing alone.  
The claim that the average factory setup cost for a frame and truss manufacturer is NZD 3 million cannot be validated with a primary New Zealand source. Capital requirements for offsite manufacturing (OSM) facilities vary widely based on material, scale, and the level of automation.5 The Commerce Commission’s market study confirmed that the requirement to invest in large-scale manufacturing plant and equipment acts as a major barrier to entry for new offsite manufacturing businesses, particularly when facing New Zealand's relatively small market size and volatile demand pipeline.5  
Industrial setup costs range from highly capital-intensive automated timber mills to localized light-gauge steel frame plants 9:

* **Large-Scale Automated Timber Facilities:** Advanced wood-processing operations require tens or hundreds of millions in capital.11 For example, Red Stag Investments committed more than NZD 200 million over a 15-year period to establish its advanced sawmill, computerized timber processing, and Cross-Laminated Timber (CLT) factory in Rotorua.11  
* **Localized Light-Gauge Steel Frame Plants:** In contrast, manufacturers of light-gauge steel framing machinery, such as Scottsdale Steel Frames and Howick Ltd, demonstrate that steel-frame manufacturing businesses are significantly more economical to set up than timber ones.9 Steel manufacturing requires a smaller physical footprint and avoids the need for heavy timber-processing machinery, allowing smaller builders to bring frame and truss production in-house for a fraction of the cost of a traditional timber factory.9

The commercial reality of high factory setup costs is illustrated by regional capacity expansions, such as the February 2026 investment by Juted Holdings Ltd, which developed a second, parallel manufacturing plant in Waihi (rebranded as Juted Frame & Truss) to meet trade demand in the upper North Island by adding automated CNC saws and a commercial framing line.13 Such capital-intensive expansions are typically only viable for established players with guaranteed merchant relationships.

## **Residential Housing Deficits and Structural Demand Drivers**

The claim of a New Zealand housing deficit of 130,000 units is a figure frequently cited in economic commentary, but it requires careful qualification. The statistic originates from two distinct economic forecasting models, representing either a historical backlog of affordable housing or a forward-looking five-year construction target:

1. **The Kiwibank Affordable Housing Deficit Model (2019):** In July 2019, Kiwibank Chief Economist Jarrod Kerr and Senior Economist Jeremy Crouchman published a report estimating a chronic supply shortage of approximately 130,000 affordable homes across New Zealand, up from 100,000 in 2018\.14 This model defined the shortage specifically within the affordable segment, representing almost 7% of the total housing stock at the time.14  
2. **The Westpac Regional Shortfall and Build Target Model (2022):** In May 2022, Westpac Senior Economist Satish Ranchhod published a bulletin stating that New Zealand would need to build approximately 130,000 new houses over the subsequent five years to clear the existing historical shortfall and keep pace with future population growth.16 This equates to a target of 26,000 new homes per year.16

Importantly, Westpac's actual estimated physical housing deficit (the static shortfall between existing households and dwellings) stood at approximately 45,000 homes nationwide in mid-2022, down from a peak of 75,000 pre-pandemic.17 This rapid reduction was driven by a temporary decline in population growth during COVID-19 border closures, coupled with a historic boom in residential consenting that peaked at over 51,000 annual consents in mid-2022.17  
Other independent modeling agencies have produced more conservative estimates of the cumulative housing shortage. The New Zealand Institute of Economic Research (NZIER) developed a national housing shortage model using Stats NZ data.19 NZIER's revised model, published in 2023, estimated that New Zealand had accumulated a cumulative shortfall of over 80,000 dwellings since 2001, which gradually eased during the pandemic due to a drop in net migration.19 Conversely, Infometrics argued in October 2019 that the net undersupply of housing nationally was closer to 40,000 homes, citing significant regional variation.15  
These regional variations are highly pronounced and directly influence local frame and truss demand:

* **Canterbury:** Following the 2011 earthquakes, Canterbury implemented rapid planning reforms and increased housing supply.19 By consistently consenting more than 500 new dwellings for every 1,000 additional residents, Canterbury successfully built its way out of its housing shortage, creating a relative surplus that stabilized local rents and house prices.15  
* **Auckland:** Due to historically strong, migration-led population growth and longer average construction completion times (often taking up to two years for a Code Compliance Certificate), Auckland has consistently held the most acute housing deficit, accounting for approximately 30,000 of the national shortage in 2019\.15

The Ministry of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), in its 2024/25 Annual Report, continues to prioritize "Going for Housing Growth," a government initiative to tackle the ongoing housing shortage by expanding land supply and e-consenting.21 Long-term forecasts suggest that dwelling consents, which bottomed out at around 30,000 per year in early 2025, will gradually recover to approximately 40,000 consents per year by 2030\.18 This projected pipeline ensures a sustained, though cyclical, demand for prefabricated structural frame and truss solutions.18

## **Workplace Safety and Injury Claim Statistics**

The construction and manufacturing sectors remain critical areas of concern for workplace safety in New Zealand.23 To monitor and evaluate safety performance, researchers analyze primary datasets from the Accident Compensation Corporation (ACC) and WorkSafe New Zealand.25  
For the 2024 calendar year, the claim that ACC accepted exactly 31,200 work-related injury claims for the construction sector, representing approximately 15% of all work-related claims nationwide, is fully validated by primary-backed construction monitoring resources.24 This corresponds with Stats NZ's provisional work-related injury statistics for 2024, which recorded a total of 209,400 work-related claims across all industries.25 A construction claim count of 31,200 represents exactly 14.89% (rounded to 15%) of this national total.24 This disproportionate injury rate is highlighted by the fact that construction workers make up only about 10% of New Zealand's total workforce.24  
An alternative, provisional ACC claim count of 28,214 accepted construction claims in 2024 (down from 32,017 in 2023\) is also cited in industry publications.23 This variation is common in ACC datasets due to standard reporting lags and the time required for claim determinations to stabilize.26  
Regarding work-related fatalities, WorkSafe New Zealand’s official register for the construction sector recorded exactly 12 fatalities between January and December 2024\.26 This matches the claimed figure. These fatalities exclude deaths from natural causes, self-harm, or long-term occupational illnesses, such as asbestos-related diseases, which are estimated to claim the lives of 220 New Zealanders annually.24

The underlying mechanisms of injury within the construction and manufacturing sectors have remained highly consistent over recent years.23 According to WorkSafe and ACC data, the primary hazards include:

* **Falls and Falling Objects:** Representing the single most dangerous hazard on site, falls from scaffolding, ladders, roofs, and elevated platforms frequently result in catastrophic trauma, fractures, and fatalities.23  
* **Power Tool Injuries:** The extensive use of high-velocity equipment, particularly nail guns, saws, and pneumatic tools, accounts for a high frequency of cuts, lacerations, and crush injuries.23  
* **Musculoskeletal Sprains and Strains:** While less acute than falls, soft-tissue injuries resulting from heavy lifting, manual handling, and awkward postures represent the most common cause of all ACC claims, leading to significant lost productivity.23  
* **The Aging Workforce Effect:** Long-term claim analysis from 2015 to 2025 reveals that while claim volumes among workers under 30 dropped by 11%, claims for workers over 70 surged by 57%.31 Older workers take substantially longer to recover and require more expensive medical treatment, driving up the average cost of construction claims to NZD 5,139 per claim by 2025\.31

Within frame and truss manufacturing facilities, the physical environment introduces specific structural risks.29 Workers predominantly operate indoors in large, noisy industrial plants, utilizing heavy automated machinery, computerized cutting saws, gantry cranes, hoists, and nail guns.29 This combination of heavy industrial equipment and manual material handling makes rigorous health and safety audits, risk assessments, and machinery guarding paramount to reducing the sector's high injury statistics.23

## **Professional Credentials and Regulatory Compliance Frameworks**

Workforce training, performance tracking, and vocational qualification structures for the frame and truss sector are managed by the Building and Construction Industry Training Organisation (BCITO), a business division of Te Pūkenga.29  
According to primary statistics published in the BCITO 2024 "Building People" report, the training registry contained exactly 15,006 active trade learners across all divisions as of December 2024\.33 However, a significant workforce deficit is apparent within the specialized prefabrication sector.33 Of the more than 15,000 active learners on the registry, only 135 were enrolled in the Frame and Truss manufacturing trade.33 Furthermore, only 32 learners successfully graduated and completed their frame and truss qualifications in 2024, highlighting a major training bottleneck within the sector.33

The industry recognizes two main qualification pathways for frame and truss professionals:

1. **New Zealand Certificate in Timber Structure Manufacturing (Level 3):** This entry-level trade qualification typically takes one year to complete.29 It provides formal recognition of practical competence in health and safety, timber properties, reading architectural plans, mathematical calculations, and operating computer-controlled cutting saws or nailing machinery.34 It includes specialized strands in Wall Frame Manufacture, Roof Truss Manufacture, and Flooring Cassette Manufacture.29  
2. **New Zealand Diploma in Timber Structure Detailing (Level 5):** This advanced technical qualification takes two to three years to complete.36 It trains individuals to operate unsupervised as frame and truss detailers.37 The curriculum is highly rigorous, covering 93 credits of construction theory, engineering principles, structural loading calculations, and the use of certified proprietary design software (such as MiTek Sapphire) to produce consented wall frame and roof truss layout plans.32

At the institutional level, the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE) introduced a major regulatory credential verification framework: the **BuiltReady** scheme.38 Established under the Building Act 2004 and the Building (Modular Component Manufacturer Scheme) Regulations 2022, the voluntary scheme officially came into force on September 7, 2022, with manufacturer applications opening in mid-2023.3  
BuiltReady serves as a streamlined, "deemed-to-comply" pathway for certified modular component manufacturers (MCMs), including prefabricated frame, truss, panel, and volumetric structure manufacturers.3 Under this legislative framework:

* **Certification and Registration:** A manufacturer must first be certified by an accredited and registered Modular Component Manufacturer Certification Body (MCMCB), such as the Joint Accreditation System of Australia and New Zealand (JASANZ).40 Following technical certification, the manufacturer must register with MBIE, satisfying a "fit and proper person" evaluation and demonstrating adequate financial means to cover potential civil liabilities.39  
* **Quality Assurance and Audits:** Certified manufacturers are audited and subjected to post-certification surveillance by their MCMCB at least once a year.39 Audits verify that the plant maintains a rigorous quality management system (QMS), documented manufacturing processes, appropriate staff training, and competency tracking.3  
* **Consenting Advantages:** Once registered, the manufacturer can issue a BuiltReady manufacturer's certificate accompanying a building consent application.3 Building Consent Authorities (local councils) must accept this certificate as draft evidence of Building Code compliance, bypassing standard factory inspections and reducing consenting processing times to just 10 working days for certified whole buildings.40

This robust certification and audit mechanism shifts civil liability for the structural integrity of the prefabricated components directly onto the registered manufacturer.40 This represents a significant regulatory evolution designed to reduce delays and lower the overall cost of housing construction in New Zealand.44

## **Sector Synthesis and Outlook**

The structural dynamics of New Zealand’s frame and truss manufacturing sector reveal deep connections between demography, capital requirements, safety, and regulatory frameworks. This synthesis connects these variables to explain the sector's current operational reality and future outlook.  
A significant challenge facing the industry is the labor bottleneck. While national economic modeling indicates New Zealand must construct 130,000 homes over a five-year period to absorb population growth and clear historical housing deficits 16, the educational pipeline to support this construction is severely constrained. BCITO data showing only 135 active apprentices in the Frame and Truss sector nationwide—compared to over 9,500 in standard carpentry—demonstrates a structural underinvestment in specialized offsite manufacturing skills.33 This shortage of qualified timber structure manufacturers and detailers limits the speed at which the industry can transition to high-productivity prefabrication methodologies.29  
This labor shortage is compounded by high capital barriers to entry, which reinforce market consolidation. Traditional timber frame and truss manufacturing requires substantial factory space, automated CNC cutting lines, and complex software licensing.5 In a small market like New Zealand, characterized by a highly volatile, "boom-bust" residential construction cycle, committing millions of dollars in capital expenditure to set up automated plants is a high-risk endeavor.5 Consequently, the industry is dominated by well-capitalized, vertically integrated merchants, such as Fletcher Building and Carter Holt Harvey.7 This consolidation was a core focus of the Commerce Commission's Residential Building Supplies Market Study, which highlighted that market concentration, restricted access to suitable land sites, and complex supplier-merchant rebate structures often limit the expansion of smaller offsite manufacturers.5  
In response to these structural inefficiencies, both the market and the government are implementing technological and regulatory solutions. Technologically, the high setup costs of timber factories have driven the adoption of light-gauge steel framing systems, which offer a lower-capital, space-efficient entry point for smaller homebuilders.9 Regulatorily, MBIE's implementation of the BuiltReady scheme represents a major shift designed to institutionalize quality control and performance tracking.38 By requiring annual third-party audits of quality systems, employee competencies, and design software, the scheme ensures that offsite manufacturers maintain high performance, while rewarding them with a streamlined, 10-day consenting pathway.3  
Furthermore, the government’s recent policy shift to accept building standards and products certified by trusted overseas jurisdictions aims to address high material costs.44 While this introduces competition and reduces the cost of structural materials, it also increases the risk of market entry by low-cost, imported prefab components.44 To maintain competitive advantages, New Zealand's domestic frame and truss manufacturers must leverage advanced digital integration—such as Building Information Modelling (BIM) and early supplier engagement models.32 This allows them to deliver highly customized, structurally verified timber solutions that minimize on-site assembly times and physical safety risks.32  
Ultimately, the New Zealand frame and truss sector is transitioning from a localized, low-volume trade into a highly regulated, digitally integrated manufacturing industry. Overcoming workforce bottlenecks, navigating high capital constraints, maintaining rigorous safety standards, and aligning with modern modular certification frameworks like BuiltReady will determine which enterprises succeed in supplying New Zealand's long-term housing pipeline.18
