Below you will find a systematic review of documented criticisms and limitations of the RIBA Plan of Work, structured according to the ten questions outlined. Each section prioritises UK-specific industry feedback, survey data, academic research, and, where available, acknowledgements from the RIBA itself.

1. The Feedback Loop Absence
There is extensive evidence that the RIBA Plan of Work historically lacks a structured mechanism for closing the performance feedback loop, a limitation widely cited as a reason for repeated design and construction failures.

The original 1963 RIBA Plan of Work included a "Stage M – Feedback," but it was removed just nine years later. Architectural theorist Frank Duffy attributes the removal to the stage’s "vague definition" and "economic ambiguity". The deficiency in feedback loops has left the homebuilding industry "lagging far behind almost all other aspects of consumption", with many new and refurbished homes found to use up to twice the amount of energy they were designed for.

The RIBA itself has acknowledged this weakness. In 2019, then‑President Ben Derbyshire stated that housing development takes place "largely in the absence of a comprehensive system for establishing performance in terms of standard setting, testing or feedback in use". He noted that "people are better informed about their breakfast cereal than they are about their homes".

More recently, industry practitioners have highlighted the absence of feedback loops as a fundamental flaw. Sam Hayes, commenting on the RIBA Engagement Overlay, noted that "the Plan of Works has its limitations, it is project bound". Similarly, Justin Kirby has observed that the Smart Buildings Overlay review is likely to produce only guidance "without a testable product that forces the Waterfall process to integrate an Agile feedback loop".

In response, the RIBA has introduced guidance such as the Plan for Use Guide (2021), which aims to embed learning lessons and feedback loops by setting measurable targets, conducting in‑use activities, and evaluating building performance. However, this supplementary guidance is not an integral part of the core Plan of Work and its adoption remains voluntary.

2. The Aspiration versus Practice Gap
Multiple surveys and practitioner accounts demonstrate a persistent gap between the theoretical intent of the RIBA Plan of Work and its actual use in UK construction projects.

Six months after the 2013 update, an Architects’ Journal (AJ) survey of 115 professionals found that just 46% had adopted the new Plan, 54% had not, and 10% said they would never use it. Moreover, 70% reported they were not being asked to use the updated work stages when bidding for new projects, indicating that clients were not demanding its use. Among small practices, only 18% were asked to use the Plan of Work in tenders, and just 34% had actually begun using it at all. Practitioner feedback at the time was blunt: "We don’t use the new Plan of Work and actually nobody we work with has even mentioned it" and "the Plan of Work is not on the radar of our typical client".

A later survey (2017) by the Association of Consultant Architects (ACA) of 73 respondents found that 52% had more problems using the numerical version than the old alphabetical stages, and 41% found the RIBA Plan of Work unhelpful in relation to the appointment of contractors. Nearly 61% said the Plan was not helpful for defining the scope of work needed for full planning applications.

The consequences of not following the stage structure are well documented. A blog from Digital Engineering Works notes that ignoring RIBA stages can lead to "inconsistencies in information exchange, causing misunderstandings and delays" and "difficulty in coordinating between disciplines, increasing the risk of errors and conflicts". Similarly, ignoring the stages is said to cause "inefficient project management".

3. The Waterfall Model Criticism
The RIBA Plan of Work is frequently characterised as a "waterfall" methodology that is inherently linear and poorly suited to the iterative, collaborative demands of modern construction projects.

Mohammad Saki of the University of Greenwich describes the Plan’s linear workflows as having stages that are "often distinct and sequential, and it might be difficult or expensive to go back after a stage is finished". He notes that "cross‑phase iterations are a rare symptom of problems, and the majority of design specifications will be locked early to prevent rework". Common planning methods like CPM and PERT "lack the ability to represent feedback and iteration" and have "come under fire for being a linear paradigm that encourages a fragmented approach to project management".

Matthew Marson, writing in his column The RIBA Plan of Doesn’t Work, contends that "the Plan of Work supports a waterfall way of working" and argues that while physical construction necessarily follows a linear sequence, digital elements do not. He observes that most construction project managers struggle to "marry these two styles". Marson also notes that starting smart building design as late as Stage 4 prevents designers from making the impact that clients expect, often leading to rework and value engineering.

Louis Koseda, in a detailed critique, states that people using waterfall methods "categorically fail to deliver projects on time, they run over budget, they aren’t satisfactory for the end‑users". He cites the CHAOS Manifesto, which found that 29% of waterfall software projects failed and 57% were challenged. Although these figures are from software, Koseda argues the same dynamics apply to construction: "Poor requirements and scope definition make up 65% of all construction project failures". He concludes that the Plan of Work "is no longer robust enough for the world we are in" and that "having this system as the core model of all professional practice restricts industrial growth and change".

4. The Information Exchange Failure
Although specific quantitative data on the proportion of projects proceeding with incomplete information is scarce, the literature consistently identifies incomplete information exchange as a predictable failure mode of the Plan of Work.

As noted above, the consequences of ignoring or failing to meet information exchange requirements include "inconsistencies in information exchange, causing misunderstandings and delays", "difficulty in coordinating between disciplines, increasing the risk of errors and conflicts", and "inefficient project management".

Legal disputes provide concrete evidence of information failures at stage boundaries. In the Technology and Construction Court case Workman Properties v. Adi Building, the contractor argued that the Employer’s Requirements, claimed to be at RIBA Stage 4, were in fact not sufficiently progressed, causing delays and significant additional cost. This case illustrates how ambiguity about the level of design completion at a stage boundary can lead directly to contractual disputes and cost overruns.

Another court case involving a dairy facility highlighted that "significant design has been developed to date which has been taken to end of RIBA Stage 4 with some parts of contractor specialist design elements together with Services design to Stage 4(i)", underscoring that incomplete or ambiguous definition of stage outputs is a recurring issue.

5. The Contractor and Specialist Engagement Problem
There is substantial evidence that the RIBA Plan of Work’s stage structure delays contractor and specialist involvement, with well‑documented consequences for buildability, cost, and programme.

Stage 4 of the RIBA Plan of Work is repeatedly identified as a "particular bottleneck". Typically, technical design only commences at this stage, and "the people who were involved in the earlier stages are unlikely to have done preliminary technical design, and don’t go on to participate in the subsequent detailing". As a result, when a manufacturer is consulted, "it might be too late for them to offer a solution that the project would benefit from".

Industry guidance warns that if specialist subcontractors are brought in too late, "there won’t be enough oversight on the most efficient ways to achieve the project goals". Similarly, the ACA survey found that 41% of respondents found the Plan of Work "unhelpful in relation to the appointment of contractors".

The Contractor Design Portions survey (2026) mapped its recommendations against RIBA stages and included analysis of "barriers to change". A related report in SpecFinish magazine identified "late delegation and ill‑defined" design responsibilities as persistent problems.

6. The Offsite and MMC Specific Criticism
There is a broad consensus that the RIBA Plan of Work, in its standard form, is poorly suited to offsite construction and Modern Methods of Construction (MMC). The 2021 DfMA Overlay has been introduced to address this, but its uptake and effectiveness have been questioned.

The fundamental problem is that MMC often requires design decisions to be made far earlier than the standard Plan of Work anticipates. As noted in New Ways of Working with MMC, "the technical design stage deliverables may need to be brought forward to as early as RIBA Stage 2". The Overlay acknowledges that "windows of opportunity" for MMC appointments vary by category—some solutions can be decided as late as Stage 4, but others must be embedded at the concept stage.

The RIBA’s own response to the House of Lords Built Environment Committee (November 2023) acknowledges that "a number of barriers" to MMC adoption persist, including lack of uptake, perception issues, and "existing funding models for housing delivery". The Institute recommends that the government "require Post‑Occupancy Evaluation on all Government funded projects involving the use of MMC".

Criticisms of the DfMA Overlay are noteworthy. Brendan Geraghty, Director at architects Geraghty Taylor, stated that "in theory the Plan of Works Overlay covers all the bases but it has not been particularly well absorbed". Academic research has found that even when attempting to adopt the DfMA Overlay, "architects design factory‑made housing within the same process as they would a traditionally constructed house", indicating that the Overlay has not fundamentally changed design behaviour.

Further, a systematic literature review on offsite construction adoption in the UK revealed that while some stages (Manufacturing, Construction, Use) have been extensively explored, design phases (Stages 2, 3, and 4) remain under‑researched.

7. The Stage 7 In‑Use Failure
Despite the reintroduction of Stage 7 "in use" in 2013, the evidence shows that formal post‑occupancy evaluation (POE) is almost universally skipped, with significant consequences for design quality and performance.

Only 19% of British architecture practices offer POE as a service. This low figure persists despite the RIBA’s own advocacy. Eleanor Jolliffe, writing in BDOnline, cites common practitioner objections: "clients just want you gone when the building is finished", "there’s a PI issue (we shouldn’t go looking for problems)", and "it’s difficult and costly to do". She argues that "can we truly say we are any good at designing buildings if we never check to make sure they work as well as we thought they would?". Jolliffe also notes that the Construction Engineering Research Laboratory found that "every dollar spent on POE resulted in ten dollars saved on operating and redesign costs".

The consequences of skipping Stage 7 are profound. The RIBA has stated that "many new and refurbished homes have been found to use twice the amount of energy aimed for". Without POE, design mistakes are repeated across projects, the energy performance gap persists, and the industry lacks the data needed to demonstrate improvement.

8. The Liability and Accountability Gap
There is evidence that the RIBA Plan of Work creates ambiguity about liability and accountability, particularly at stage boundaries, and that this ambiguity has contributed to disputes and calls for clearer duty‑holder roles.

The most significant acknowledgement of this shortcoming came after the Grenfell Tower tragedy. The RIBA’s proposed Plan of Work for Fire Safety (2018) was explicitly designed to address "a lack of duty‑holders responsible for implementing fire safety measures". The Hackitt Review had called for "greater transparency, accountability and collaboration from industry". The RIBA itself stated that the Fire Safety overlay provides "much‑needed clarity on fire‑safety roles and responsibilities at every stage of the process". However, one industry professional described the proposal as "lightweight and embarrassing", and others questioned who should take responsibility for key design and inspection tasks.

The misalignment between the Plan of Work and standard forms of contract is also cited as a source of disputes. John Ridgett of Design Engine noted that "other members of the team have continued to reference the ‘old’ plan of work, meaning that fees and deliverables across the project become uncoordinated". For example, a structural engineer might quote based on RIBA Stage D (old system) while the architect quotes to Stage 3 (new system), creating immediate inconsistency.

Legal cases further illustrate accountability problems. In the dairy facility case, ambiguity about whether the design had reached Stage 4 became a central point of dispute. In Workman Properties v. Adi Building, disagreement over the actual level of design completion at Stage 4 led to claims of delay and significant extra cost.

9. The Small Project and Non‑Architect Criticism
The RIBA Plan of Work is widely perceived as being designed primarily for large, architect‑led projects, with practitioners reporting that it is overly complex and prescriptive for smaller projects or projects where the architect’s role is limited.

A 2024 guide notes as a con that "RIBA’s complexity isn’t always necessary for smaller projects and can be overwhelming for first‑time renovators" and that "the plan requires significant documentation and it can be overly time‑consuming to follow all the procedures". Amazon customer reviews of the Small Projects Handbook complain that the book is "based on rigidly following the RIBA plan of work, which is pretty bloated for very small projects", noting that "there are, for example, over 80 pages of text before the handbook suggests that any design work starts".

The RIBA itself has acknowledged this limitation. In response to feedback, the Institute published a "RIBA Plan of Work for Small Projects". However, this recognition of the need for a separate version implicitly confirms that the core framework is not suitable for smaller projects.

10. The Data and Evidence Generation Failure
There is compelling evidence that the RIBA Plan of Work does not generate structured performance data that could be used to improve future projects, and this absence is a significant barrier to industry‑wide learning and improvement.

The RIBA has itself acknowledged this failure. In its 2019 statement, the Institute noted that housing development takes place "in the absence of a comprehensive system for establishing performance in terms of standard setting, testing or feedback in use". The Institute observed that "the dearth of feedback loops in the built environment generally, and housing in particular, has left the homebuilding industry lagging far behind almost all other aspects of consumption".

Academic research has explored this gap. A study by ARCOM (2025) questioned "the appropriateness of the RIBA Plan of Work for the delivery of low‑energy housing" and found that "the RIBA Plan of Work did not hold the key to incorporating sustainability into the design process". Other researchers have attempted to integrate circularity indicators into the Plan of Work framework, precisely because the framework itself does not inherently capture such data.

The consequences of this data vacuum are significant. Without structured performance data on cost, programme, quality, and carbon, the industry cannot demonstrate improvement over time. The reliance on voluntary POE (only 19% of practices) means that the small amount of data that is collected is not standardised and cannot be aggregated for industry‑wide learning. As one building performance analysis put it, the lack of a continuous loop "from in‑use to design and back to in‑use" prevents systematic improvement.

Synthesis and Concluding Observations
The evidence reviewed reveals a consistent pattern: the RIBA Plan of Work is widely respected as a normative framework for project organisation, but it is frequently not followed in practice. Its linear, waterfall structure clashes with the iterative, collaborative nature of modern construction, particularly in relation to digital integration, offsite manufacturing, and MMC. The framework’s absence of a closed feedback loop means that learning from completed projects is rare, and the almost universal skipping of Stage 7 perpetuates this problem. Information exchanges at stage boundaries are often incomplete, leading to rework, disputes, and cost overruns. Contractor and specialist involvement is routinely delayed until Stage 4, causing buildability and value engineering failures. For smaller projects, the framework is widely seen as overly complex and prescriptive, and it fails to generate the structured performance data needed for industry‑wide improvement.

The RIBA has acknowledged some of these limitations through supplementary guidance, overlays (e.g., DfMA, Fire Safety), and a separate Small Projects framework. However, these responses are incremental rather than foundational, and uptake has been uneven. As one practitioner concluded, the Plan of Work "is no longer robust enough for the world we are in" and "having this system as the core model of all professional practice restricts industrial growth and change".

While thorough documentation of its shortcomings is plentiful, evidence detailing the RIBA Plan of Work's strengths and successes is more limited, often taking the form of described benefits rather than objective data. The following analysis presents the known positive attributes, acknowledged strengths, and active improvements to the framework as identified by the industry, academia, and the RIBA itself, structured according to the ten themes from the previous critique.

1. Feedback Loop Reinforcement
While the framework has been historically criticised for weak feedback mechanisms, the RIBA has taken deliberate steps to address this. The 2021 Plan for Use Guide was specifically introduced as an overlay to foster a culture of learning and continuous improvement, with core components of setting targets, implementing activities, and measuring building performance. The 2020 update is also described as being built on "nearly seven years of feedback gathered from the construction industry".

2. Framework Adoption and Recognition
Despite survey data revealing lower-than-expected adoption rates, the Plan of Work is universally recognised as the industry's primary reference. It is frequently cited by major organisations like NHS England as an "industry-standard methodology" and is regarded as "the industry standard framework for running projects". In practice, it continues to be used as the central roadmap for complex and large-scale projects. Furthermore, studies in Sri Lanka are exploring the "critical success factors of time, cost and quality performance" for projects applying the RIBA Plan of Work, indicating its international relevance.

3. Adaptation From a Waterfall Model
The RIBA Plan of Work is gradually evolving from its traditional "waterfall" approach. The 2020 update introduced more flexible stage definitions, particularly at the Stage 3 and 4 interface, allowing for "different approaches to the timing of planning applications" and recognising that for MMC, some technical design decisions may need to be made earlier. The framework's overlays, such as the DfMA Overlay, are designed to help architects start thinking about buildability and offsite methods at Stage 2, promoting more iterative collaboration between design and manufacture.

4. Structured Information Exchange
The Plan of Work's design provides a formal structure for information flow, defining "core tasks and information exchanges across eight stages of the design and construction process". This standardised approach establishes a clear "chain of custody for quality," helping all project members understand their risks and responsibilities. The framework is seen as "a coordination and communication tool" that improves collaboration between clients, architects, consultants, and contractors.

5. Contractor and Specialist Integration
The framework is actively used to improve the integration of specialists earlier in the project life cycle. The 2023/2024 Engagement Overlay is designed to "provide a standardised approach" for professionals to enhance the quality of engagement with all stakeholders, creating "capacity for public participation beyond traditional methods". New editions of the RIBA Job Book and standard contracts are continuously updated to better reflect a collaborative working environment.

6. Offsite and MMC Suitability
The core limitation of the Plan of Work for MMC is being directly addressed. The creation of the "Designing for Manufacture and Assembly (DfMA)" Overlay was a significant positive step, designed to "increase architects' knowledge and understanding of offsite methods". Adopting a DfMA approach is believed to offer substantial benefits, including a "20%-60% reduction in construction programme time" and significant reductions in "energy consumption during the construction process and reductions in onsite waste".

7. Embracing Stage 7: In-Use
The RIBA has acknowledged the importance of post-occupancy evaluation (POE). It reintroduced "Stage 7 – Use" in the 2013 update, and its own research shows that "POE makes greener, cheaper, more productive buildings". The Plan of Work provides a direct framework for conducting these evaluations, ensuring they are a formalised project deliverable rather than an afterthought.

8. Liability and Accountability Frameworks
While the framework can create ambiguity, it also provides a foundation for defining roles. The RIBA has updated its contracts (e.g., the RIBA PSC 2020) "to align with the new revised RIBA Plan of Work," helping to standardise how liability and professional indemnity insurance are addressed within the project stages. The framework's stage-gate structure is seen as establishing a logical progression for defining responsibilities and deliverables.

9. Adaptability for Small Projects
The RIBA has recognised the need for a more flexible approach for smaller commissions by publishing the Small Projects Handbook. This guide provides "simple step-by-step guidance to the key tasks in that stage of the Plan of Work," tailored for projects typically under £250,000, making the framework accessible and less bureaucratic for smaller practices.

10. Enabling Data and Evidence Generation
The Plan of Work is evolving into a tool for evidence-based decision-making, notably in sustainability. The 2020 Plan integrates a "Sustainability Strategy" to help project teams "plan buildings that meet the highest environmental standards". The framework is also enabling the development of advanced analytical tools, such as the Landscape Institute's carbon tool, which provides "practical checklists to help cut emissions throughout the lifecycle of a project" as an overlay to the Plan of Work. By embedding lean strategies into the Plan of Work 2020, researchers are creating frameworks for enhancing "time, cost, and sustainability performance".

Summary of Positive Evidence
The positive evidence for the Plan of Work primarily highlights its role as a widely-recognised industry standard that provides a common language and structured framework for projects. While adoption is not universal, its influence is pervasive through standards, contracts, and government guidance. Crucially, the RIBA has demonstrated a capacity to adapt the framework by introducing new overlays (e.g., DfMA, Smart Buildings, Fire Safety) and supplementary guides (e.g., Plan for Use, Small Projects Handbook) in direct response to identified industry limitations. This iterative process of updating the Plan based on nearly seven years of feedback is, in itself, a testament to its living, evolving nature as a tool for the entire construction industry.

Note: In contrast to the detailed critique, the positive evidence is less focused on quantitative success metrics and more on qualitative strengths, industry recognition, and the framework's acknowledged potential for improvement through its own system of overlays and updates.
