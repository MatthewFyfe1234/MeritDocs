# **Forensic Report on Liability Attribution, Regulatory Evolution, and Process Verification in Mobile Elevating Work Platform Catastrophes**

Determining fault in Mobile Elevating Work Platform (MEWP) accidents has historically been one of the most litigated and structurally complex areas of modern occupational health and safety law.1 Because these incidents often occur at the interface of manufacturer software logic, rental company maintenance regimes, employer schedule pressures, and operator physical execution, establishing a clear sequence of events is highly challenging.3 In the absence of an objective, tamper-proof digital "process record"—such as logged telemetry of outrigger load, pre-use inspection sequences, control activation, or wind speeds—liability disputes between these parties frequently persist for years.3  
These disputes incur millions of dollars in legal fees and prevent timely resolution for injured parties and insurers.8 Consequently, the global powered access industry, under the guidance of bodies like the International Powered Access Federation (IPAF) and national regulators like the UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) and US Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), has increasingly shifted away from subjective witness testimony toward sensor-based verification and telemetry.10

## **UK Health and Safety Executive Prosecutions and Calibration Failures**

Within the UK jurisdiction, HSE prosecutions have targeted the intersection of electronic calibration, unapproved structural modifications, and the failure of independent certification regimes.4 These cases illustrate how the absence of digitized logs of software changes and inspection sequences results in prolonged legal battles and catastrophic structural failures.2

### **The Kimberly Access M25 Overturn: Genie UK Ltd**

On June 5, 2013, Rick Jeager-Fozard, an experienced yard foreman at Kimberly Access Limited’s Iver depot, was conducting a routine pre-delivery inspection (PDI) on a brand new Genie Z135/70 articulating boom platform.4 As he elevated the platform, the machine extended to an unsafe angle, lost stability, and toppled over a boundary fence, crashing onto the hard shoulder of the adjacent M25 motorway.4 Jeager-Fozard fell with the basket and died from massive trauma.4  
The subsequent HSE forensic investigation revealed a severe calibration defect in the machine’s secondary boom angle sensor.4 High-reach MEWPs rely on precise electronic angle and length data to calculate the safe outreach envelope and prevent structural tipping.13  
In this case, the secondary boom angle sensor was miscalibrated, registering that the boom had raised to its safe threshold when it was actually approximately six degrees lower than required.4 Consequently, when the extension function was selected, the secondary boom extended beyond its physical limit, shifting the center of gravity outside the machine's stability footprint and initiating the tip-over.4

The primary legal dispute centered on how this miscalibration occurred.4 The HSE found that incorrect calibration parameters had been manually manipulated and uploaded to the machine's controller via a laptop.4 This software interface, WebGPI, was password-protected and managed under the direct authority of Genie UK Limited (a subsidiary of Terex) as part of their warranty and commissioning undertaking.4  
Genie UK Ltd attempted to shift liability to Kimberly Access by arguing that a thorough PDI should have caught the physical angle deviation.4 However, because Genie UK Ltd maintained absolute control over the proprietary software and executed the warranty repairs during this period, the prosecution established that they were the sole entity responsible for introducing the compromised calibration profile.4  
The litigation took nine years to reach a court conclusion.6 In July 2022, at Reading Crown Court, Genie UK Ltd pleaded guilty to breaching Section 3(1) of the Health and Safety at Work Act 1974\.6 The manufacturer was fined **£270,000** and ordered to pay **£165,175** in prosecution costs.6

### **The Buchanan House Structural Collapse: Craig Services & Access Ltd**

On July 20, 2012, at Buchanan House in Glasgow, safety net rigger Gary Currie and self-employed operator Alexander Nisbet were working at a height of approximately 30 meters in the basket of a vehicle-mounted cherry picker owned by Craig Services & Access Limited.2 Without warning, the third section of the main boom buckled, causing the basket to plummet 92 feet to the ground, killing Currie and inflicting life-changing head and brain injuries on Nisbet.2  
The HSE forensic reconstruction uncovered a history of unauthorized modifications and fraudulent inspections.2 Fourteen months prior, on May 17, 2011, the same cherry picker's third boom section had buckled during an operation in Penicuik.2 At that time, Craig Services engaged in a prolonged dispute with the Italian manufacturer's representative, CTE Spa, over liability.2  
Because CTE Spa insisted on a complete replacement of the damaged boom section, Craig Services bypassed the manufacturer.2 Donald Craig, the manager of Craig Services, commissioned an independent welding firm to carry out a major, unapproved structural repair and modification on Boom 3\.2 Craig falsely assured the welders that the machine would be physically restricted to operate only as a "short-reach" platform where Boom 3 would remain nested inside Boom 2 and never bear load.2 However, Craig Services made no such mechanical or control modifications, and the cherry picker was returned to full-height service.2  
To secure statutory certification, Donald Craig presented misleading information to John McCallum of J M Access Solutions Ltd.18 McCallum, failing to conduct a load test or demand manufacturer-approved drawings, issued a statutory safe-to-use certificate based on a visual inspection.18 Three months later, the unapproved, structurally compromised weld failed under normal operating loads.19  
At Airdrie Sheriff Court, the prosecution secured historic criminal convictions.18 Donald Craig was found guilty under Section 37(1) of the Health and Safety at Work Act 1974 and sentenced to the maximum **two years in prison**.19 Craig Services (in liquidation) was fined **£61,000**, and J M Access Solutions Ltd (which ceased trading) was fined **£30,000**.18 This case demonstrated that visual-only inspection regimes are wholly inadequate for verifying structural repairs on complex high-reach equipment.18

### **The Belfast Loading Failure: Balloo Hire Centre Ltd**

On October 4, 2022, on Hamilton Road in Belfast, a large self-propelled MEWP was being loaded onto a lorry trailer.35 The MEWP was equipped with adjustable and retractable axles, which significantly alter the machine's track width and stability footprint during transport setup.36 During the loading operation, the MEWP toppled off the side of the trailer.35 The operator was ejected from the platform onto the public road, sustaining life-changing injuries.35  
The subsequent investigation by the Health and Safety Executive for Northern Ireland (HSENI) focused on the plant hire company's risk assessment and operational supervision.36 HSENI identified that Balloo Hire Centre Ltd had failed to identify the specific stability risks associated with loading plant fitted with retractable axles.36 Furthermore, the company failed to provide a trained banksman to assist the operator, failed to segregate live public traffic from the loading area, and failed to secure a safe system of work.36  
In September 2024, at Laganside Crown Court, Balloo Hire Centre Ltd pleaded guilty to breaching Articles 4(1) and 5(1) of the Health and Safety at Work (Northern Ireland) Order 1978 and was fined **£80,000**.36 This prosecution highlighted that rental companies hold a strict, non-delegable duty to manage the physical risks of transport, loading, and machine configuration, even when the operator is highly trained.36

## **US OSHA Investigations and Civil Product Liability Jurisprudence**

The United States product liability framework features intense litigation regarding the balance between "design defects" (Strict Liability) and "operator negligence" (Contributory Negligence).3 Manufacturers frequently attempt to shift liability to the operator's employer by citing OSHA training standards.37 However, federal and state appellate courts have increasingly scrutinized whether a machine's design actively facilitates human error when safer, passive alternative designs exist.3

### **The optional Guarding Systems Liability: Wilda v. JLG Industries**

The case of *Wilda v. JLG Industries, Inc.* (2021) in the US District Court for the Northern District of Illinois represents a major battle over the legal significance of optional safety features.3 On January 30, 2015, ironworker Patrick C. Wilda was operating a JLG 460SJ telescoping boom lift to install steel roof joists at a warehouse construction site.3 While maneuvering the basket in reverse, Wilda was struck from behind by a steel roof beam.3 The collision pinned him forward onto the platform's control console, initiating "sustained involuntary operation of controls".3 The joystick, held in the active position by his pinned body, continued to drive the basket upward, crushing and asphyxiating him.3  
Wilda’s estate filed a strict product liability and negligence suit against JLG, alleging the control console was defectively designed because it lacked an active secondary guarding system to prevent sustained involuntary activation.3 JLG launched a multi-layered legal defense 3:

* JLG sued the equipment distributor, Illini Hi-Reach, Inc., arguing that JLG offered a patented secondary guarding system called "SkyGuard" as an option.3 Because Illini Hi-Reach owned several SkyGuard kits but chose not to install one on this specific rental unit, JLG argued that liability for the defect shifted entirely to the distributor.3  
* Illini Hi-Reach, in turn, sued the employer, Area Erectors, Inc., alleging that the employer failed to conduct a proper site-specific risk assessment or request secondary guarding.3  
* JLG moved for summary judgment, asserting that Wilda's own "misuse" of the lift—specifically, failing to look in the direction of travel as commanded by the operator's manual—was the sole proximate cause of the crash.3

US District Judge Steven C. Seeger denied JLG's motions to exclude the plaintiff's engineering experts and denied JLG's motion for summary judgment on all counts.3 The court established that under Illinois strict product liability law, a manufacturer cannot delegate its duty to design a safe product to a distributor or end-user simply by offering safety equipment as an optional purchase.3  
The court further ruled that operating a boom lift near overhead steel structures is the precise, foreseeable purpose of the machine.3 Therefore, any failure by the operator to look behind them constitutes contributory negligence, which does not bar recovery under strict liability, rather than an unforeseeable "misuse" of the product.3

### **The Limits of Foreseeable Misuse: Genie Industries, Inc. v. Matak**

The absolute boundaries of a manufacturer's liability for operator misuse were defined by the Supreme Court of Texas in the case *Genie Industries, Inc. v. Matak* (2015).1 On March 30, 2009, Walter "Pete Logan" Matak, an employee of Gulf Coast Electric, was working from a Genie AWP-40S aerial work platform at a church in Beaumont.1 The AWP-40S is a lightweight, stationary lift supported by four outrigger legs with adjustable leveling jacks.7 To prevent tipping, the machine features an electrical interlock system: the platform cannot be raised unless all four outriggers are locked in place and fully supporting weight.7  
Because lowering the platform, removing the outriggers, moving the lift, and redeploying the outriggers was highly time-consuming, a church employee named John Adams suggested a shortcut.7 He advised the crew to keep Matak fully elevated 40 feet in the air, raise the leveling jacks just an inch or two to clear the carpet, and manually push the 1,000-pound machine to the next spot.7 While Matak was 40 feet aloft, his co-workers raised two of the outriggers.1 The machine immediately became unstable, tipped over, and crashed onto the sanctuary floor, killing Matak from massive head injuries.7  
Matak's family sued Genie, alleging strict product liability for design defect.1 They argued that Genie could have utilized several safer alternative designs, including 7:

* An **Automatic Drop-Down system** that would immediately lower the basket if an outrigger was released.7  
* **Pothole Protection bars** to catch the chassis and limit the tipping angle.7  
* A **Chain and Padlock system** to physically lock the outriggers, with the key held only by a supervisor.7

A Jefferson County jury found Genie 55% liable, which was affirmed by the Court of Appeals.1 However, the Texas Supreme Court reversed and rendered judgment for Genie.1  
Applying the "risk-utility" test, Chief Justice Nathan Hecht ruled that the AWP-40S was not unreasonably dangerous as designed.1 The court emphasized that a product is not defective simply because it can be deliberately destabilized.7 The lift's immense utility as a lightweight, highly portable machine that can fit through standard doors would be destroyed by heavy, complex automated outriggers.7  
Crucially, because the danger of moving a 40-foot elevated lift was "open and obvious," and backed by explicit "DANGER: Tip-over hazard" decals on the chassis, and because Genie had sold over 100,000 units with only three reported similar misuses over a decade, the court held that the utility of the design far outweighed the risk of such extreme, warned-against misuse.1

### **Active vs. Passive Safety Designs: Doyle Sappington v. Skyjack, Inc.**

The civil product liability dispute in *Doyle Sappington v. Skyjack, Inc.* (2011) illustrates the transition from active, operator-reliant safety checks to passive, machine-enforced safety systems.32 Doyle Sappington, a carpenter, was operating a Skyjack Model SJII 4626 scissor lift on a smooth concrete surface with a minimal slope of 0.07 percent.32 When the work platform was raised to its maximum working height of 26 feet, one of the wheels dropped into a small depression, causing the scissor lift to lose stability and tip over, resulting in Sappington's death.32  
Sappington’s family brought a strict products liability claim against Skyjack and the equipment lessor, RSC, alleging the SJII model was defective and unreasonably dangerous because it lacked "pothole protection".32 The SJIII model, manufactured two years later, was equipped with automatic pothole protection bars that deployed mechanically when elevated.32  
Skyjack argued that the SJII complied with the ANSI standards in effect at the time of its manufacture in 1995 and that the accident was caused by the operator's failure to identify local floor hazards.32 The plaintiffs presented expert testing from Bryan Johnson and Dr. Blundell, showing that the newer SJIII pothole protection design successfully prevented tip-overs under identical depression drops and was both economically and technologically feasible in 1995\.32  
The US Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit reversed the district court's summary judgment in favor of Skyjack.32 The court ruled that under Missouri law, a plaintiff can establish a strict products liability claim by proving a design is unreasonably dangerous through the existence of a feasible alternative design, and that such stability failures do not require expert testimony to be understood by a jury.32 This case forced manufacturers to recognize that compliance with historical ANSI standards does not immunize them if a safer, feasible design is available.32

### **Foreseeable Human Failures: Camacho v. JLG Industries, Inc.**

The legal framework surrounding manual versus automatic safety interlocks was further refined in *Camacho v. JLG Industries, Inc.* (2023).34 Camacho, a commercial glass installer, was working from a JLG scissor lift.34 The lift's entrance was guarded by a manual safety chain.34 Camacho failed to latch the chain while working.34 During the installation of a heavy glass panel, he lost his balance and fell through the unguarded entrance, sustaining severe, permanent injuries.34  
Camacho sued JLG, alleging the scissor lift was defectively designed because it utilized a manual chain rather than a spring-loaded, self-closing gate with a built-in toeboard.34 JLG argued that the risk of falling through an open entrance was open and obvious and that Camacho’s failure to hook the chain was the sole proximate cause of his injuries.34 JLG’s counsel argued that "a defect in the chain design could not have caused the injury because Camacho did not use the chain".34  
The California Court of Appeal rejected JLG’s argument, ruling that Camacho’s theory of liability was not that the chain itself was broken, but that the *scissor lift as designed with a chain was inherently defective because it was highly foreseeable that users would fail to latch it*.34 The court noted that JLG manufactured an alternative model equipped with a spring-loaded, self-closing gate and toeboard, which eliminated human error for an additional manufacturing cost of only **$154**.34  
The court held that a manufacturer cannot escape liability for a manual safety device if a low-cost, passive, automatic alternative exists that completely eliminates the possibility of human omission.34

## **Coronial Inquests and the "Process Record" Gap**

In Australia, the UK, and New Zealand, coronial inquests serve as vital legal mechanisms for identifying systemic gaps between a manufacturer's specified operating procedures and what the operator actually did.22 These inquests frequently identify the absence of a verified, objective "process record" as a major obstacle to both safety management and liability resolution.5

### **The Newstead Crane Truck Overturn: Christopher Ian Powell**

On December 14, 2015, professional photographer Christopher Ian Powell was hoisted approximately 50 meters above a vacant development lot at 70 Longland Street in Newstead, Brisbane, to capture marketing photographs for a planned high-rise tower.22 He was positioned in a crew basket raised from a 33-ton truck-mounted EWP equipped with a 70-meter telescopic boom on a turntable supported by four outrigger legs with stabilizing pads.22  
After 20 minutes aloft, the rear passenger-side outrigger suddenly punched 1.7 meters into the ground, which had been weakened by excavation, demolition, and recent heavy rain.22 The opposing outriggers lifted off the ground, the entire EWP rotated 46 degrees, and the massive boom crashed onto the public street.22 Powell was killed instantly.22

 \---\> \---\> \---\> \---\> \[Overturn\]

At the Brisbane Coroners Court, the subsequent inquest conducted by Coroner Donald MacKenzie exposed a critical gap in high-capacity EWP operations.22 Although the EWP was fitted with an advanced stability computer, this system could only detect outrigger displacement and minor chassis inclination; it could not calculate or warn of sudden soil shear failure.22  
The inquest revealed that EWP operators were routinely forced to assess ground-bearing pressure based on a subjective "gut feeling" or visual inspection.22 Because no verified process record of ground-bearing pressure or geotechnical certification existed prior to setup, liability was hotly disputed between the equipment owner (Lincon Logistics), the developer (Tom Dooley Developments), and the operator.22  
Coroner MacKenzie issued a landmark recommendation: the Queensland Government must mandate that an independent civil engineering report certifying ground stability must be completed prior to the operation of any EWP with a boom exceeding 11 meters where ground surety is unknown.22 This recommendation represented a major shift from operator-reliant assessments to objective, engineering-certified process records.22

### **The Royal Adelaide Hospital Crushing Inquest: Jorge Alberto Castillo-Riffo**

On November 27, 2014, Jorge Alberto Castillo-Riffo, an experienced construction worker employed by SRG Building (Structural Systems), was operating a scissor lift at the New Royal Adelaide Hospital (NRAH) construction site.5 While working in a confined overhead area, Castillo-Riffo was crushed between the scissor lift’s guardrail and a concrete floor slab above, sustaining catastrophic brain injuries from which he died the following day.26  
The incident triggered a massive, highly politicized liability battle.5 SafeWork SA initially launched prosecutions against the immediate employer SRG and the principal contractor, the Hansen Yuncken Leighton Contractors (HYLC) joint venture.24 SRG entered into a court-sanctioned enforceable undertaking to improve safety procedures.28 However, on February 10, 2017—the business day before the trial was scheduled to begin—SafeWork SA dropped all charges against the HYLC JV without explanation, sparking public outrage and allegations of government-backed cover-ups.24  
The subsequent coronial inquest conducted by State Coroner Mark Johns exposed systemic failures in the "he-said, she-said" liability environment of construction sites 23:

* The inquest revealed a culture of "wallpaper safety," where perfect digital and physical safety paperwork (Safe Work Method Statements) was generated to meet legal requirements, while the actual conditions on the ground were rushed and highly dangerous due to extreme schedule pressures.5  
* Because there was no ground-support spotter or verified process log of the operation, Castillo-Riffo remained trapped, crushed against the controls, for up to 30 minutes before a passing worker discovered him.28  
* When rescuers attempted to lower the platform, they struggled to find or operate the emergency manual lowering valve because the valve was poorly marked, unstandardized, and physically awkward to access.26

Coroner Johns issued a sweeping recommendation that scissor lifts must not be operated on any construction site unless a fully trained, competent "safety spotter" is positioned on the ground at all times to activate the emergency lowering controls.26 This recommendation sparked intense debate within the global powered access industry, with trade bodies warning that mandating a second worker for every scissor lift could drive contractors back to using highly dangerous ladders and scaffold towers.26

## **Civil Contract Disputes and Generic Aftermarket Equipment Risks**

While criminal prosecutions focus on regulatory violations, civil contract litigation between operators and rental companies often turns on the precise definition of "satisfactory quality" and the forensic consequences of destroyed evidence.31

### **Non-OEM Component Supply and Evidence Spoliation: Baxter v. Barnes**

The case of *Baxter v. Barnes* (2015) in the High Court of England and Wales represents a critical warning regarding the supply of non-OEM outrigger pads and the legal doctrine of evidence spoliation.30 Christopher Baxter, a highly qualified and experienced arborist, hired a specialized "Spider" type MEWP from defendant WE Barnes Tree Surgeons to prune a large copper beech tree on a residential sloping property.30  
The Spider platform is specifically designed to operate on slopes and rough terrain, provided its outriggers are set up on reasonably level, stable ground.30 Together with the machine, the defendant supplied four hard plastic plates (bearing pads) to place under the outrigger feet.30 While Baxter was working in the basket, the machine juddered, became unstable, and tipped over, throwing Baxter and his employee to the ground and causing severe, life-changing spinal and skeletal injuries.30  
The core of the legal battle involved the physical interaction between the outrigger feet and the plastic plates.30 The defendant argued that Baxter was negligent because his employees had set the outriggers on a slope that was too steep.30 The plaintiff’s forensic experts argued that the accident was caused because the outrigger feet physically slipped off the edge of the bearing plates.30  
During the trial, it was revealed that the hard plastic plates supplied by the defendant were not original manufacturer parts but were generic aftermarket plates.30 Expert testimony established that because the Spider platform is subjected to significant lateral forces during boom rotation, the bearing plates must be physically attached to the outrigger feet or feature deep, molded recesses to prevent the outrigger from slipping off the plate.30 The generic plates supplied were completely flat and smooth.30  
A major legal complication arose because the defendant had completely destroyed and disposed of the crashed Spider lift before the plaintiff's engineering experts had an opportunity to inspect its safety interlocks and electronic tilt sensors.30 The plaintiff demanded that the court draw an "adverse inference" against the defendant under the precedent *Malhotra v. Dhawan*, which establishes that if a party is responsible for the unavailability of critical evidence, the court should make assumptions against that party’s interest.30  
Although the High Court declined to issue a formal adverse inference (concluding the disposal was negligent rather than a deliberate attempt to gain a legal advantage), the court ruled heavily in favor of the plaintiff on the balance of probabilities.31 The court held that under the Supply of Goods and Services Act, the rental company was in breach of contract because the MEWP was not of "satisfactory quality".30  
The supply of generic, flat plastic plates that were incapable of resisting the lateral forces of a Spider lift constituted a fundamental defect in the equipment package.30 The operator was cleared of all contributory negligence.30

## **Insurance Underwriting, Telematics, and the Financial Impact of Unresolved Disputes**

For the global insurance market—including syndicates within Lloyd’s of London, Allianz, AXA, and Zurich—the MEWP sector has historically represented a high-frequency, severe liability exposure.51 When a MEWP incident occurs, the absence of a verified, tamper-proof process record almost guarantees that the subsequent litigation will be prolonged and economically inefficient.6  
Insurers have identified that the legal cost of resolving a single fatal MEWP dispute frequently exceeds the actual compensation value.8 For example, in the *Jeager-Fozard* case, the legal proceedings took nine years, during which extensive forensic engineering reconstruction, digital software analysis, and witness depositions cost the parties millions in fees before a plea was entered.4  
Similarly, in the *Castillo-Riffo* case, the dropping of criminal charges and subsequent coronial inquiries ran for over four years, while the victim's family was subjected to a fragmented compensation process that failed to provide timely closure or clear fault attribution.5  
This economic reality is driving insurance risk managers to move away from purely reactive litigation defense and toward mandating active, machine-level risk mitigation technology.12 Insurers are increasingly partnering with major construction companies to offer lower general liability and worker's compensation premiums if they implement specific technologies 52:

* **Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS/Drones):** AXA XL and other major commercial insurers actively encourage the use of specialized LiDAR and thermal-imaging drones to conduct structural and window inspections on complex building envelopes.52 By eliminating the use of a physical manlift for routine inspections, contractors eliminate operator exposure to fall, crushing, and wind-loading hazards.44  
* **Active Telematics and Data Logging:** Insurance risk managers are advocating for the integration of standard, tamper-proof telematics systems.53 Systems such as the *OverWatch* secondary guarding system, which incorporates a robust, integrated digital data logger capable of storing more than 10,000 distinct machine operations (covering 3 to 6 months of typical on-site usage), are viewed by underwriters as the equivalent of a commercial aircraft's "black box".12

By logging real-time operator inputs, platform loads, outrigger status, wind speeds, and safety sensor states, the data logger establishes a definitive process record.12 If an incident occurs, the insurer can immediately determine whether the failure was due to a manufacturer's software calibration anomaly, a rental company's failed maintenance sequence, or an operator's deliberate bypass of safety controls.3 This immediate clarity eliminates the prolonged "he-said, she-said" legal battles, dramatically reducing insurance defense costs and ensuring that safety-critical lessons are immediately integrated into global operational practices.9

## **Strategic Conclusions and Forensic Recommendations**

A holistic review of the litigation, coronial findings, and regulatory evolutions in the powered access industry indicates that the historic model of assigning MEWP liability is functionally obsolete. The traditional model relies on post-incident forensic reconstruction and witness testimony, which is easily manipulated, frequently destroyed, and inherently prone to prolonged disputes.5  
The future of MEWP safety and legal risk transfer lies in the complete digitization of the operating sequence.12 By mandating active safety control systems (such as platform load sensing, dynamic terrain monitoring, and secondary guarding) and pairing them with tamper-proof telematics data loggers, the industry can eliminate both the human-error gaps that lead to fatalities and the lack of process records that makes liability attribution an endless, costly battleground.3
